Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome)

Runner's knee is an ache around or behind the kneecap that flares with stairs, squats and running, and targeted hip and knee exercise is its best-supported treatment.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: patellofemoral pain syndrome, PFPS, patellofemoral pain, anterior knee pain

What is Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome)?

Your kneecap, or patella, glides in a groove at the end of your thighbone as you bend and straighten your leg. Patellofemoral pain is an ache around or behind the kneecap that worsens when that joint is loaded in a bent position. The APTA's clinical practice guideline describes it as pain that usually begins without a specific injury and builds with squatting, stairs, prolonged sitting, jumping or running.

Despite the nickname, you do not have to be a runner to get it. Mayo Clinic notes that it mainly affects adolescents and young adults and that females are twice as likely to develop it. Running and jumping sports, sudden increases in training, weakness around the hip and knee, and previous knee surgery all raise the odds. It can affect one knee or both.

Common symptoms

  • Dull, aching pain around or behind the kneecap
  • Pain going up or down stairs
  • Pain with squatting, kneeling, running or jumping
  • An ache after sitting for a long time with the knees bent
  • A popping or crackling feeling in the knee
  • Pain that comes on gradually, often without a specific injury

Causes and risk factors

  • Overuse from running and jumping sports
  • Sudden increases in how long, how often or how hard you train
  • Weak hip and thigh muscles that affect how the kneecap tracks
  • Kneecap or leg alignment differences
  • Previous knee surgery, especially ACL reconstruction using the patellar tendon
  • Being female, an adolescent or a young adult

How physical therapy helps

The APTA's 2019 clinical practice guideline for patellofemoral pain recommends exercise therapy that combines hip and knee strengthening to reduce pain and improve function in the short, medium and long term. It supports combining exercise with options such as taping, kneecap mobilization or stretching, and it recommends against knee braces, sleeves and straps and against passive treatments like ultrasound and electrical stimulation. The exercise you do is the core of the plan.

Hip strengthening

Exercises for the muscles at the back and side of the hip. The APTA guideline notes hip-targeted exercise has shown benefit in the short and medium term, which can help when loading the knee is still painful.

Knee strengthening

Quadriceps work such as resisted squats or knee extensions, progressed as the joint behind the kneecap tolerates more load.

Patellar taping

Tape applied to the kneecap may be added to exercise for short-term pain relief, according to the APTA guideline, to make training more comfortable.

Running gait retraining

For runners, cues to adjust step rate, foot strike or hip motion, which the guideline lists as an option for easing load on the kneecap.

Foot support guidance

If your feet roll in more than usual, the guideline supports prefabricated shoe inserts combined with exercise for short-term pain relief, up to about six weeks.

Load management education

A plan for how much running, jumping and stair work to do while symptoms settle, so you stay active with fewer flare-ups.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist asks when the pain started, what aggravates it and whether your training recently changed. Squatting and stair stepping are key checks, since the APTA guideline uses pain during these movements to identify patellofemoral pain. We measure hip and thigh strength, check quadriceps, hamstring and calf flexibility, look at how the kneecap moves, and watch your control on one leg. Runners may be asked about or shown their stride.

Recovery

Patellofemoral pain often responds to a consistent exercise program, but it can be stubborn. The APTA guideline notes that symptoms can recur and persist for years, and that a longer history of pain, higher pain levels and poorer function at the start are linked with slower recovery. The same guideline reports that combined hip and knee exercise reduces pain and improves function over the long term. Keeping up your home exercises after formal therapy ends is one of the most practical ways to limit recurrences.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Scale back the activities that bring on the ache, such as hills, deep squats and stairs, rather than stopping all exercise
  • Try lower-impact options like stationary cycling or swimming, as the AAOS suggests
  • Ice the front of the knee for about 20 minutes after activity if it helps
  • Stand up and move regularly instead of sitting for long stretches with your knees sharply bent
  • Increase running distance or intensity gradually once you return

When to see a doctor right away

  • Knee pain that is not improving after a few days of rest, or a knee that is getting harder to move (Mayo Clinic)
  • Marked or sudden swelling
  • The knee locks, gives way or clicks painfully (NHS)
  • Pain that began with a specific fall or twisting injury
  • Fever with redness, warmth or swelling around the knee
  • Pain that keeps you from bearing weight

Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome): common questions

What is the fastest way to heal runner's knee?
There is no shortcut, but the approach with the strongest evidence is a combined hip and knee strengthening program. The APTA's 2019 clinical practice guideline recommends it to reduce pain and improve function in the short, medium and long term. Pairing exercise with smart load management, and short-term aids like taping when they help, tends to work better than rest or passive treatments alone.
Can I keep running with runner's knee?
Many people can keep running in some form while they rehab, as long as distance, pace and terrain are adjusted so pain stays mild and settles by the next day. Hills and stairs are often the first things to reduce. The APTA guideline also lists gait retraining, such as increasing step rate or changing foot strike, as an option for runners with patellofemoral pain.
Does a knee brace help runner's knee?
The evidence does not support it. The APTA's 2019 clinical practice guideline recommends that clinicians not prescribe patellofemoral braces, sleeves or straps for patellofemoral pain. Patellar taping is different: the same guideline says taping combined with exercise may reduce pain in the short term. Strengthening the hip and thigh muscles remains the main treatment.
Why does my knee hurt going down stairs?
Going down stairs loads the joint between your kneecap and thighbone while the knee is bent and your thigh muscles work to control the descent. The APTA's clinical practice guideline lists stair climbing and descent, along with squatting, among the activities that typically reproduce patellofemoral pain. Building hip and quadriceps strength usually makes stairs easier over time.
Is runner's knee the same as IT band syndrome?
No. Runner's knee, or patellofemoral pain, is felt around or behind the kneecap at the front of the knee. IT band syndrome causes pain on the outside of the knee where the iliotibial band crosses the thighbone. Both are common in runners and both respond to hip strengthening, but the exam and exercise emphasis differ, so an evaluation helps sort out which one you have.
How long does runner's knee last?
It varies widely. Some people improve within weeks of starting a structured exercise program, while others have symptoms for much longer. The APTA's 2019 guideline notes that 50% to 56% of adolescents still report knee pain two years after diagnosis, and that people with longer-standing or more severe pain tend to recover more slowly. Starting targeted exercise sooner may help.
