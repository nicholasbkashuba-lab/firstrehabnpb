Ankle

Chronic Ankle Instability

If your ankle keeps rolling or feels ready to give out, targeted balance and strength training is the first-line approach in physical therapy guidelines.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: CAI, recurrent ankle sprains, functional ankle instability, mechanical ankle instability, weak ankle that gives way

Chronic Ankle Instability, explained

Chronic ankle instability describes an ankle that keeps giving way or feeling unsteady long after a sprain. The 2021 JOSPT clinical practice guideline defines it as perceived instability or episodes of giving way that last more than 12 months after the first injury and limit your activity. InformedHealth, published on the NIH Bookshelf, uses an earlier marker: an ankle that still gives way too easily six months after the sprain.

Two overlapping problems are usually involved. Mechanical instability means the ligaments healed too loosely. Functional instability means the ankle's position sense and muscle reactions are too slow to catch a roll. A 2019 review in the Journal of Athletic Training reports that up to 70% of people who sprain an ankle may go on to develop lasting instability, and it is more common in people who run, jump and change direction.

Common symptoms

  • The ankle giving way, especially on uneven ground or during sport
  • Repeated sprains, sometimes from small missteps
  • A constant feeling that the ankle is wobbly or unreliable
  • Ongoing pain, tenderness or swelling on the outer ankle
  • Stiffness when bending the ankle upward
  • Cutting back on sports or activities because you do not trust the ankle

Causes and risk factors

  • A previous lateral ankle sprain, one of the strongest risk factors for another
  • Ligaments that healed in a stretched or loose position
  • Reduced balance and position sense after injury
  • Weakness in the muscles that steady the ankle
  • Sports with running, jumping and cutting, as well as dance and gymnastics
  • Stopping rehab before balance and strength fully returned

How physical therapy helps

Exercise is the first-line treatment. The 2021 APTA clinical practice guideline in JOSPT gives its highest grade to proprioceptive and neuromuscular exercise for improving dynamic stability and how stable the ankle feels during daily activity. The same guideline supports joint mobilization to improve ankle bending and balance in the short term, and advises against using a brace or tape alone to fix balance. InformedHealth recommends training for about 20 to 30 minutes three or four times a week for at least 4 to 6 weeks.

Progressive balance training

Single-leg standing that advances to softer surfaces, eyes closed, reaching and weight shifts, so the ankle relearns to react quickly to a misstep.

Neuromuscular and strength work

Exercises that build strength and coordination in the muscles around the ankle and lower leg, done in the clinic and as a home program.

Joint mobilization

Hands-on mobilization and mobilization with movement to restore the ankle's upward bend, which the guideline links to better balance in the short term.

Brace planning

Advice on when a removable brace makes sense for higher-risk activity, used alongside training rather than in place of it.

Sport and work readiness

Late-stage drills that test cutting, landing and uneven-ground walking before you return to full activity.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit takes about an hour, and treatment begins that day. Your therapist goes through your history of sprains and the situations where the ankle gives way, then checks ligament looseness, ankle motion (especially bending upward), strength and single-leg balance. Reaching or hopping tasks may show how the ankle handles load. The findings guide whether rehab alone is a good fit or whether a physician should also take a look.

Recovery

Training effects can show up early. InformedHealth notes that balance and strength exercise can improve ankle stability and movement within the first few weeks, though programs usually run at least 4 to 6 weeks. Some people with very loose ligaments still have giving-way episodes despite good rehab, and a surgeon may then discuss tightening or rebuilding the ligaments. If you have surgery, therapy follows your surgeon's protocol, and InformedHealth reports that starting exercise within two to three weeks after surgery helped people return to work and sport sooner.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Practice standing on the affected leg for short periods each day, holding a counter for safety.
  • Wear a removable ankle brace for sports or hikes while you rebuild strength and balance.
  • If the ankle rolls again, protect it and use ice, compression and elevation for the first couple of days.
  • Take extra care on uneven ground, stairs and in low light until your balance improves.

When to see a doctor right away

  • After a new roll, you cannot put weight on the foot or walk more than a few steps
  • You heard a crack, or the ankle looks misshapen
  • Numbness or tingling, or a foot that turns blue or grey or feels cold to the touch
  • Pain or swelling after a sprain that is not improving over several weeks
  • Giving-way episodes that continue despite several weeks of consistent rehab

Chronic Ankle Instability: common questions

Will a weak ankle get stronger on its own?
Many sprained ankles do settle without lasting trouble. The 2021 JOSPT guideline cites a study in which 60% of people had recovered by 12 months, while 40% developed chronic ankle instability. Ankles that keep giving way usually need specific balance and position-sense training, which the guideline recommends at its highest grade.
Do I need surgery for chronic ankle instability?
Most people with chronic ankle instability start with exercise therapy, which the JOSPT guideline recommends at its highest grade. InformedHealth notes that surgery may be considered when the ligaments remain too loose, either to tighten them or to replace them with a tendon graft. A surgeon makes that decision, and therapy after surgery follows the surgeon's protocol.
Should I wear an ankle brace all the time if my ankle is unstable?
A brace can protect an unstable ankle during sport or rough terrain, but the 2021 JOSPT guideline advises against relying on braces or tape alone to improve balance in chronic ankle instability. Bracing works best alongside balance and strength training. Many people wear one for higher-risk activities and go without it for everyday walking once their control improves.
What exercises help an unstable ankle?
For chronic ankle instability, InformedHealth describes neuromuscular and proprioceptive training such as foot movements, standing on one leg and shifting weight, which build strength, balance and coordination. Exercises usually start on firm ground and progress to softer surfaces, eyes closed or sport-like movements. A physical therapist can set the starting level and decide when to progress.
Can chronic ankle instability lead to arthritis?
It can raise the risk. A 2019 review in the Journal of Athletic Training reports that lateral ankle sprains contribute to 13% to 22% of all ankle osteoarthritis cases and 80% of posttraumatic ankle osteoarthritis. Mayo Clinic also lists ankle arthritis as a possible complication of sprains. Reducing repeat sprains is one reason to treat instability rather than living with it.
Is ankle instability more common in athletes and dancers?
Yes. A 2019 review in the Journal of Athletic Training found chronic ankle instability more common in people who do running, jumping and cutting sports, and noted that dancers and gymnasts may have even higher rates. Female athletes reported it nearly twice as often as male athletes (32% compared with 17%). Sport-specific balance work is often part of rehab for these groups.
