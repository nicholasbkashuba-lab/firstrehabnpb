Cubital Tunnel Syndrome

When your ring and little fingers go numb with the elbow bent, hand therapy with a custom night splint may help calm the irritated ulnar nerve.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: ulnar nerve entrapment at the elbow, ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, CuTS, funny bone nerve compression

Cubital Tunnel Syndrome, explained

Your ulnar nerve runs down the arm and passes behind the inside of the elbow through a narrow channel called the cubital tunnel. This is the spot people call the funny bone. The nerve has little padding there, so bending the elbow stretches it and leaning on the elbow presses on it. Over time that pressure can cause numbness, tingling and weakness in the hand.

A 2022 review in the Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online calls it the second most common nerve compression problem in the arm and estimates it affects about 1.8% of people in the US. It often shows up in people who keep the elbow bent for long periods, lean on their elbows, or have had an elbow fracture, dislocation or arthritis. In many cases no single cause is found.

Common symptoms

  • Numbness and tingling in the ring and little fingers
  • Symptoms that come and go, often worse with the elbow bent
  • Waking at night with a numb hand
  • Aching on the inside of the elbow
  • Weaker grip and clumsiness with fine tasks
  • Fingers that feel like they have fallen asleep
  • Thinning of the hand muscles in long-standing cases

Causes and risk factors

  • Keeping the elbow bent for long stretches, such as when driving, holding a phone or sleeping
  • Leaning on the elbow on desks, armrests or car doors
  • A previous elbow fracture or dislocation
  • Arthritis, bone spurs or cysts near the elbow
  • The nerve snapping over the bony bump as the elbow bends
  • Repetitive pulling, reaching or lifting, and baseball pitching

How hand therapy helps

For mild to moderate cases, conservative care comes first. A 2019 systematic review in Orthopedic Reviews found moderate-strength evidence for education with activity changes and for splinting, and the two appeared about equally effective. A 2022 review in the Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online reports that earlier studies found 50% to 88% success with nonoperative care. At First Rehabilitation, cubital tunnel care is led by Laura Drumm, CHT, and custom splints are fabricated in the clinic.

Custom night splint

A splint molded to your arm in the clinic holds the elbow in a straighter position while you sleep, so the nerve is not stretched for hours. AAOS and Johns Hopkins Medicine both list a night splint or padded brace among nonsurgical options.

Posture and habit coaching

Practical changes for the desk, car, phone and bed that cut down time spent with the elbow sharply bent. Reviews rate this kind of education as effective as splinting for mild to moderate cases.

Pressure relief

Elbow padding and workstation adjustments keep weight off the inner elbow, where the nerve sits close to the surface.

Nerve gliding exercises

Gentle, coordinated movements of the elbow and wrist that help the ulnar nerve slide through its tunnel. They are a common option, although research support is weaker than for splinting.

Sensation and strength tracking

Your therapist rechecks sensation, pinch and grip at follow-up visits, and any worsening is shared with your physician promptly.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour with treatment the same day. Your therapist reviews when the numbness started and which positions bring it on, then checks feeling in the ring and little fingers, grip and pinch strength, and the small hand muscles for thinning. Tapping over the nerve at the elbow (Tinel's sign) shows whether it is irritable. Splint needs are assessed, and you leave with a home program.

Recovery

Nerves recover slowly. Cleveland Clinic notes that recovery can take many months because nerves heal more slowly than other tissues. In a randomized trial described in the 2022 Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online review, about 90% of people with early disease improved with education, night splints or nerve glides. If numbness becomes constant, muscles weaken, or conservative care does not help, your physician may discuss surgery. After surgery, therapy follows your surgeon's protocol, and the clinic coordinates with your surgeon.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Avoid leaning on your elbow, and pad hard armrests or the car door.
  • Limit long periods with the elbow sharply bent; use a headset or speakerphone for long calls.
  • Keep the elbow straighter at night when you can, since AAOS notes that sleeping with bent elbows aggravates symptoms.
  • Take breaks from tasks that involve repeated pulling, reaching or lifting.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Numbness that becomes constant instead of coming and going
  • Hand muscles that look thinner, or a grip that keeps getting weaker
  • Increasing clumsiness or trouble coordinating your fingers
  • Symptoms that last more than six weeks or are severe

Cubital Tunnel Syndrome: common questions

Can cubital tunnel syndrome go away on its own?
Mild cubital tunnel syndrome often improves once the pressure on the ulnar nerve is reduced. A 2022 review in the Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online notes that people with intermittent symptoms and no weakness or muscle wasting are candidates for nonoperative care, with past studies reporting 50% to 88% success. Changing habits and splinting at night tend to matter more than waiting it out.
What is the best sleeping position for cubital tunnel syndrome?
With cubital tunnel syndrome, the goal at night is keeping the elbow fairly straight. AAOS notes that sleeping with bent elbows aggravates symptoms, and Johns Hopkins Medicine recommends a splint or foam elbow brace worn at night to limit bending. Try not to sleep with your hands tucked under the pillow or your arms folded tightly against your chest.
Why are my ring and little fingers numb?
The ulnar nerve supplies feeling to the little finger and half of the ring finger, according to AAOS. Numbness in those fingers often points to irritation of that nerve, and the elbow is a common place for it to be compressed. The nerve can also be squeezed at the wrist in a passage called Guyon's canal, so an evaluation helps sort out where the problem is.
Is a custom splint better than a store-bought elbow brace for cubital tunnel?
Both aim to stop the elbow from bending sharply during sleep, which is what helps the ulnar nerve. A custom splint is molded to your arm, so the angle, padding and straps can be set for your comfort and adjusted as symptoms change. At First Rehabilitation, splints for cubital tunnel syndrome are fabricated in the clinic by the hand therapy team.
Are nerve gliding exercises safe for cubital tunnel syndrome?
Nerve gliding exercises are gentle movements meant to help the ulnar nerve slide through the cubital tunnel, and AAOS and Cleveland Clinic list them as a treatment option. A 2019 systematic review found the research support weaker than for splinting or education. They should feel easy and should not leave your fingers more numb afterward, so it helps to learn them from a hand therapist first.
When is surgery needed for cubital tunnel syndrome?
AAOS says your doctor may recommend surgery for cubital tunnel syndrome when nonsurgical treatment has not helped, the ulnar nerve is very compressed, or the compression has caused muscle weakness or damage. Symptoms that are severe or have lasted more than 6 weeks are a reason to see your doctor. If you do have surgery, hand therapy afterward follows the surgeon's protocol, and First Rehabilitation coordinates with your surgeon.
