De Quervain's Tenosynovitis

Sharp pain on the thumb side of your wrist when you lift, pinch, or twist? Hand therapy rests the irritated tendons and changes how you load them.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: De Quervain's tendinosis, De Quervain's syndrome, radial styloid tenosynovitis, thumb-side wrist tendonitis

De Quervain's Tenosynovitis, explained

Two tendons that move your thumb, the abductor pollicis longus and the extensor pollicis brevis, travel together through a short tunnel on the thumb side of the wrist. The tunnel sits over a bony bump at the end of the forearm bone called the radial styloid. In De Quervain's tenosynovitis, the tunnel lining swells or thickens, so the tendons rub as they slide. Gripping, pinching, and turning the wrist then become painful.

The AAOS reports that it is most common in people in their 40s and 50s and affects more women than men. New mothers are a well-known group. The AAOS notes symptoms often appear within 4 to 6 weeks after delivery, and Mayo Clinic links repeated lifting of a baby with the thumbs to higher risk. Childcare workers, manual laborers, and people with rheumatoid arthritis can develop it too.

Common symptoms

  • Pain on the thumb side of the wrist
  • Pain that travels up the forearm or into the thumb
  • Pain with forceful gripping, lifting, or twisting the wrist
  • Swelling near the base of the thumb
  • A catching or snapping feeling when you move the thumb
  • Less motion in the thumb and wrist

Causes and risk factors

  • Repetitive thumb and wrist movements such as typing, texting, or lifting
  • Lifting a baby or young child with the thumbs spread
  • Pregnancy and the months after delivery
  • Being female and over about 40
  • Rheumatoid arthritis or other inflammatory conditions
  • A wrist injury that leaves scar tissue

How hand therapy helps

Therapy for De Quervain's starts by quieting the irritated tendons, then finds and changes the movements that keep provoking them. Mayo Clinic notes that a physical or occupational therapist can look at how you use your wrist, suggest ways to reduce strain, and teach strengthening exercises. The AAOS lists splinting and activity changes as first-line care. Mayo Clinic notes that therapy also aims to keep symptoms from coming back, which is why we pair the splint with changes in how you use your hand.

Custom thumb spica orthosis

Laura Drumm, CHT, fabricates a forearm-based splint in the clinic that holds the thumb and wrist still so the two tendons can rest, molded for night wear and aggravating tasks.

Activity and lifting changes

Mayo Clinic advises avoiding pinching or gripping while moving the wrist. We work out new ways to lift a child, hold a phone, or use tools that follow that rule.

Education on provoking positions

You learn which combinations load the tunnel most, such as pinching while the wrist bends toward the little finger, so you can avoid them while healing.

Gradual strengthening

Once pain settles, exercises rebuild wrist, hand, and forearm strength, as Mayo Clinic describes, so the tendons tolerate everyday loads again.

Coordination with your physician

If symptoms do not respond, we share findings with your physician, who may consider an injection or surgery, and we provide therapy afterward following their protocol.

What to expect at your first visit

Plan on about an hour for evaluation and treatment. Laura checks for tenderness and swelling on the thumb side of the wrist and may use the Finkelstein test, which the AAOS describes as tucking the thumb into the palm and bending the wrist toward the little finger. She measures thumb and wrist motion and pinch and grip strength, screens the thumb base joint for arthritis, and fits your splint.

Recovery

Mayo Clinic reports that when treatment begins early, symptoms typically improve within 4 to 6 weeks. Cases that have been present longer, or that keep getting stirred up by the same tasks, can take more time, which is why activity changes matter as much as the splint. The AAOS notes that most patients do very well with treatment. If therapy and splinting do not settle things, your physician may suggest an injection or surgery, and therapy afterward helps you regain motion and strength.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Rest the thumb from the motions that hurt, especially pinching or gripping while you twist or bend the wrist.
  • Ice the painful area for about 15 minutes at a time; the AAOS suggests repeating this every 4 to 6 hours.
  • Use your whole hand rather than a spread thumb when you lift a child or a heavy object.
  • An over-the-counter anti-inflammatory may help if your doctor approves; Cleveland Clinic advises checking with a provider before taking one for more than 10 days.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Pain that does not improve after rest, ice, and over-the-counter pain relievers (Mayo Clinic)
  • Wrist pain that started with a fall, since a fracture needs to be ruled out
  • Numbness or tingling over the back of the thumb or hand, which can come from an irritated nerve rather than the tendons
  • Pain that keeps getting worse during or after activity (Cleveland Clinic)

De Quervain's Tenosynovitis: common questions

How long does De Quervain's take to heal?
Mayo Clinic reports that when treatment starts early, De Quervain's symptoms typically improve within 4 to 6 weeks. Symptoms that have lasted many months, or that keep getting aggravated by lifting and pinching, often take longer. Healing time also depends on how consistently you wear a splint and change the movements that strain the thumb-side tendons. A hand therapist can track your progress and adjust the plan.
Should I wear a De Quervain's brace all day or just at night?
It depends on how irritable your tendons are. The AAOS recommends a removable splint that keeps the wrist straight and the thumb still, and Cleveland Clinic says most people wear a splint or brace for a few weeks. A brace that includes the thumb and wrist is typical. Your hand therapist sets a schedule, often including aggravating daytime tasks, and tapers it as pain eases.
Why does De Quervain's happen after having a baby?
The AAOS notes that De Quervain's symptoms often appear within 4 to 6 weeks after delivery. Mayo Clinic points to two likely reasons: fluid changes from pregnancy hormones, and repeatedly lifting an infant with the thumbs spread, which strains the two thumb tendons. Changing how you lift and feed your baby, plus a splint, are common parts of hand therapy for new parents.
What is the Finkelstein test?
The Finkelstein test is a simple exam used to check for De Quervain's tenosynovitis. According to the AAOS, you place your thumb in your palm, close your fingers over it, and bend your wrist toward your little finger. Pain on the thumb side of the wrist suggests the condition may be present. It is a screening test, and a clinician interprets the result alongside other findings.
Is De Quervain's the same as thumb arthritis?
No, although both cause pain near the thumb. De Quervain's involves two tendons sliding over the radial styloid on the thumb side of the wrist. Thumb arthritis is cartilage wear in the joint at the base of the thumb. Because thumb base arthritis can cause pain in a nearby spot, a careful exam is needed to tell them apart.
Do exercises help De Quervain's tenosynovitis?
Yes, once the tendons are calmer. Mayo Clinic notes that a physical or occupational therapist can teach exercises to strengthen the wrist, hand, and arm, and Cleveland Clinic mentions grip exercises. Timing matters, because loading painful tendons too early can flare them. Most programs start with rest and a splint, then add gentle motion and progressive strengthening as symptoms allow.
