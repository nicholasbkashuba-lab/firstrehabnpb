Shoulder Dislocation and Instability

Physical therapy after a dislocated shoulder, or for a shoulder that feels loose, focused on strength, control and a safe return to what you love doing.

Also called: dislocated shoulder, shoulder subluxation, recurrent shoulder dislocation, multidirectional instability, loose shoulder

Shoulder Dislocation and Instability, explained

The shoulder gives up some stability in exchange for a huge range of motion. The ball at the top of your upper arm bone sits against a small, shallow socket and is held there by the labrum, ligaments and the four rotator cuff muscles. A dislocation happens when the ball comes fully out of the socket. A subluxation is a partial slip. Once a shoulder has dislocated, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes it is more vulnerable to repeat episodes.

Most dislocations go out the front of the joint, often when the arm is raised away from the body, and they commonly tear the front of the labrum (a Bankart lesion). Other people have shoulders that are naturally loose in several directions, called multidirectional instability, which shows up in swimmers, volleyball and tennis players. According to Mayo Clinic, dislocations occur most often in people in their teens and 20s, particularly contact athletes.

Common symptoms

  • A shoulder that looks out of place or misshapen after an injury
  • Intense pain and trouble moving the arm
  • Swelling or bruising around the shoulder
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm
  • A feeling that the shoulder slips, shifts or gives out
  • Fear or apprehension when the arm is raised and turned outward
  • Clicking or popping with pushing, pulling or overhead activity

Causes and risk factors

  • A fall onto the arm or a hard hit in a contact sport
  • Motor vehicle accidents
  • A previous dislocation, which raises the chance of another
  • Naturally loose ligaments (hyperlaxity)
  • Repetitive overhead motion in sports like swimming, tennis and volleyball
  • Younger age, especially males in their late teens
  • Seizures or electric shock

How physical therapy helps

For both first-time and recurring instability, therapy works on making the muscles around the shoulder better at holding the ball in the socket. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists strengthening and shoulder control work as nonsurgical treatment for chronic instability. NCBI StatPearls describes a common path after a first dislocation: brief protection for pain in the first 1 to 2 weeks, then restoring full motion, strengthening the rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles, and sport-specific drills before return to play.

Protected range of motion

Guided movement as pain settles so the shoulder does not stiffen, weaning from the sling as your physician allows.

Rotator cuff strengthening

Progressive resistance for the four rotator cuff muscles, which press the ball into the socket and act as active stabilizers of the joint.

Shoulder blade stabilization

Exercises for the muscles that position the shoulder blade, a focus of therapy for both traumatic and multidirectional instability.

Joint awareness and muscle retraining

Drills that sharpen your sense of where the arm is and how quickly the muscles respond, so the shoulder holds steady in positions that used to feel risky.

Sport and work-specific training

A gradual return to throwing, swimming, lifting or contact, using interval programs with scheduled rest.

Post-surgical rehab

After a stabilization procedure, care follows your surgeon's protocol and the clinic coordinates with the surgeon. The American Physical Therapy Association notes a sling for 4 to 6 weeks after surgery.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist will go over how the shoulder first came out, how many times it has happened since, and which positions feel unsafe. The exam looks at range of motion, rotator cuff and shoulder blade strength, and muscle control. Gentle stability tests, such as the apprehension and relocation tests, help show which directions are involved. Sensation and strength down the arm are checked as well, since nearby nerves can be injured in a dislocation.

Recovery

Timelines depend on whether this is a first dislocation, how much the labrum or bone was damaged, and what you need the shoulder to do. Age matters a great deal. According to NCBI StatPearls, adolescents aged 14 to 16 had re-dislocation rates of 37% to 42% in one analysis, and being under 20, playing contact or overhead sports, and having loose ligaments all raise the risk. After stabilization surgery, the American Physical Therapy Association describes four to six months before full return to sports and heavy activities.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Avoid the position that tends to provoke the shoulder, usually the arm raised overhead and rotated backward, until you have been evaluated.
  • Use an ice pack wrapped in a towel to calm soreness after activity.
  • Once a physician has confirmed the shoulder is back in place, move it gently within a comfortable range.
  • Hold off on contact sports and heavy overhead lifting until a therapist or physician clears you.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Your shoulder looks deformed or out of place. Go to an emergency room or urgent care and do not try to put it back yourself.
  • Numbness in the arm or hand, or a change in skin color, after an injury
  • You cannot move your arm
  • Severe swelling, bruising or intense pain after a fall or collision
  • Repeated episodes of the shoulder coming out, which may need an orthopedic surgeon's opinion

Shoulder Dislocation and Instability: common questions

How long should I wear a sling after a dislocated shoulder?
It depends on how the shoulder is treated. When a dislocation is managed without surgery, NCBI StatPearls notes that the ideal length of immobilization is still debated, and many protocols use a sling briefly for comfort in the first 1 to 2 weeks. After stabilization surgery, the American Physical Therapy Association describes a sling for 4 to 6 weeks. Follow the timeline your physician gives you.
Can physical therapy prevent another shoulder dislocation?
No treatment can guarantee it. Physical therapy builds the rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles, improves control of the joint, and teaches you which positions and movements put the shoulder at risk, all of which the American Physical Therapy Association describes as ways to reduce reinjury. Torn tissue and bone loss do not change with exercise, which is why some shoulders still need surgery.
What is the difference between a shoulder dislocation and a subluxation?
In a dislocation, the ball of the upper arm comes completely out of the socket and usually needs a medical professional to put it back. In a subluxation, the ball slides partway out and often slips back into place on its own. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, both are forms of shoulder instability, and either one can leave the joint feeling loose or prone to slipping again.
What is multidirectional instability of the shoulder?
Multidirectional instability means the shoulder joint is loose enough to slip forward, backward or downward, often without a major injury. The American Physical Therapy Association notes it is common in athletes with naturally loose joints, especially swimmers and pitchers, and less common after 40 as tissues stiffen. Treatment usually centers on strengthening the rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles and improving control.
When can I return to sports after a dislocated shoulder?
The answer depends on whether you had surgery. NCBI StatPearls describes some in-season athletes treated without surgery returning in about 2 to 3 weeks under close supervision, once motion, strength and sport drills allow. After stabilization surgery, the American Physical Therapy Association puts full return to sports at four to six months. Your therapist will use strength and functional tests to guide the decision.
Do I need surgery for shoulder instability?
Not everyone does. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons recommends surgery when a dislocation cannot be put back in place or when the shoulder keeps dislocating. NCBI StatPearls notes earlier surgery is often considered for people under 20, contact and overhead athletes, and shoulders with significant bone loss. Many others are managed with a structured strengthening program first.
