Knee

ACL Tear

After an ACL tear, physical therapy helps calm the knee and rebuild strength and stability, whether you are managing without surgery or preparing for it.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: torn ACL, anterior cruciate ligament tear, ACL sprain, ACL injury

ACL Tear, explained

The anterior cruciate ligament runs diagonally through the middle of your knee, connecting the thighbone to the shinbone. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, its job is to keep the shinbone from sliding out in front of the thighbone. Injuries are graded from a mild stretch (grade 1) to a partial tear (grade 2) to a complete tear (grade 3), in which the knee becomes unstable.

The AAOS reports that ACL injuries affect more than 200,000 people in the United States each year and that female athletes are injured at 2 to 8 times the rate of males. Most tears happen without contact, during cutting, pivoting, sudden stops or awkward landings. Mayo Clinic lists soccer, basketball, football, volleyball, gymnastics and skiing as higher-risk sports. A direct blow to the knee can also tear the ligament.

Common symptoms

  • A loud pop or popping feeling at the moment of injury
  • Severe pain and difficulty continuing the activity
  • Rapid swelling, usually within 24 hours
  • A feeling that the knee is unstable or gives out
  • Loss of full range of motion
  • Tenderness along the joint line
  • Discomfort while walking

Causes and risk factors

  • Sudden change of direction or slowing down quickly
  • Pivoting with the foot firmly planted
  • Landing awkwardly from a jump
  • A direct blow or collision to the knee
  • Being a female athlete
  • Poor conditioning, muscle fatigue or faulty landing technique
  • A previous ACL injury

How physical therapy helps

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons states that non-operative management is always an option and includes working with physical therapy. In a randomized trial in the New England Journal of Medicine (Frobell and colleagues, 2010), young active adults who began with structured rehabilitation and had the option of later reconstruction had similar two-year results to those who had early surgery, and more than half of them had not had reconstruction by two years. When surgery is planned, Mayo Clinic notes that physical therapy beforehand helps strengthen the knee.

Swelling and motion recovery

Early work to settle the knee and regain full straightening and bending, which the APTA lists among the first goals after injury and before surgery.

Quadriceps and hamstring strengthening

Progressive weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing exercises to rebuild thigh strength, which drops quickly after an ACL injury.

Neuromuscular and balance training

Balance, perturbation and movement-control drills. The APTA knee ligament guideline recommends pairing neuromuscular re-education with strengthening for knees with stability problems.

Landing and cutting mechanics

Coaching on how to land, stop and pivot with good hip and knee control, the same skills Mayo Clinic describes in ACL prevention programs.

Prehab before surgery

If reconstruction is planned, we work on swelling, motion and quadriceps strength beforehand and keep your surgeon informed of your progress.

Bracing discussion

The APTA guideline says clinicians may use functional bracing for knees without an intact ACL, so we help you and your doctor weigh whether one fits your activities.

What to expect at your first visit

We review how the injury happened, whether you felt a pop, and which activities you want to return to. Your therapist measures swelling, knee range of motion and quadriceps strength, and watches how you walk and bear weight. Gentle hands-on stability tests, such as the Lachman test, help show how the ligament is functioning. We also check hip and ankle strength and coordinate the plan with your physician or surgeon.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the grade of the tear, any other injuries in the knee, and what you need the knee to do. Mayo Clinic notes that a non-surgical approach can work well for people without knee instability, including those with partial tears or lower activity demands. For athletes returning to cutting and pivoting sports after reconstruction, Mayo Clinic says it can take a year or more to return safely, and the APTA notes it may take over a year to regain the strength, stability and power to play. If you have surgery, your surgeon's protocol guides each phase.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Follow rest, ice, compression and elevation in the first days after the injury
  • Ice for about 20 minutes at a time, as often as every two hours, as Mayo Clinic suggests
  • Use crutches if walking is painful or the knee feels unstable
  • Avoid pivoting, cutting and jumping until the knee has been evaluated
  • Prop the leg on pillows when resting to help reduce swelling

When to see a doctor right away

  • A pop with rapid swelling after an injury, since Mayo Clinic advises prompt care because cartilage or meniscus may also be damaged
  • You cannot bear weight on the leg
  • The knee gives out or feels unstable
  • A visible deformity of the leg or knee after the injury
  • The knee is locked and will not straighten
  • Severe pain associated with an injury

ACL Tear: common questions

Can you recover from an ACL tear without surgery?
Some people can. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says non-operative management is always an option, and Mayo Clinic notes it can work well for people whose knee is not unstable, including partial tears and lower activity demands. In a 2010 New England Journal of Medicine trial, more than half of young active adults who started with structured rehabilitation had not had reconstruction two years later, and their results were similar to those who had early surgery.
Can you walk on a torn ACL?
Right after the injury, walking is often painful, and the American Physical Therapy Association notes the knee usually swells within minutes to hours. Once swelling settles, many people can walk in a straight line, but the knee may feel loose or give way with turning. Walking on an unstable knee can risk further damage, so it is worth having the knee evaluated before you return to sports.
What is ACL prehab?
Prehab is physical therapy before ACL surgery. The American Physical Therapy Association describes it as reducing swelling, increasing knee range of motion and strengthening the quadriceps before reconstruction, and Mayo Clinic notes that physical therapy before surgery helps strengthen the knee. Going into the operation with a calm, mobile, stronger knee gives you a better starting point for rehabilitation, which then follows your surgeon's protocol.
How long after an ACL tear can you return to sports?
For athletes who have reconstruction, Mayo Clinic says it generally takes a year or more before it is safe to return to play, and the American Physical Therapy Association notes it may take over a year to regain strength, stability and power. Without surgery, timing depends on whether the knee stays stable during sport-specific drills. Return is usually based on strength, hop and movement testing rather than the calendar alone.
Is a partial ACL tear treated differently from a full tear?
Often, yes. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons grades a partial tear as grade 2, where the ligament is stretched and loosened, while a grade 3 tear is complete and leaves the knee unstable. Mayo Clinic notes that non-surgical care can work well for partial tears. The plan depends on how stable your knee is and what you want to do, which your doctor and therapist assess together.
Can ACL tears be prevented?
The risk can be reduced. The American Physical Therapy Association reports that an analysis of multiple studies found a 67% reduction in non-contact ACL injuries in females who took part in prevention programs. Those programs ran at least six weeks, two to three times per week, and included strengthening, balance and landing practice. Mayo Clinic also recommends core and hamstring strengthening and proper jumping and pivoting technique.
