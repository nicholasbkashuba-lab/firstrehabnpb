Shoulder Labral Tear (SLAP Tear)

Physical therapy for a torn shoulder labrum, with one-on-one care focused on rebuilding strength, control and confidence in your shoulder.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: SLAP tear, SLAP lesion, superior labrum anterior to posterior tear, torn shoulder labrum, glenoid labrum tear

What is Shoulder Labral Tear (SLAP Tear)?

Your shoulder is a ball and socket joint with a shallow socket. A ring of firm cartilage called the labrum lines the rim of that socket, deepening it so the ball of the upper arm bone fits more securely. The labrum also anchors ligaments and one end of the biceps tendon. A SLAP tear is damage to the top part of the labrum, where the biceps attaches, and the tear runs from front to back.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, SLAP tears happen in two main ways: a sudden injury, such as a fall on an outstretched arm or a forceful pull on the arm, or repeated overhead motion. Throwing athletes and weightlifters are especially prone. In adults over 30 to 40, some fraying of the labrum can be a normal part of aging.

Common symptoms

  • A deep ache inside the shoulder, or sharp pain with certain movements
  • Pain when lifting or reaching overhead
  • Locking, popping, catching or grinding in the joint
  • A feeling that the shoulder is unstable or might slip
  • Reduced strength and range of motion
  • In throwing athletes, lost velocity or a 'dead arm' feeling

Causes and risk factors

  • Falling onto an outstretched arm
  • A sudden, forceful pull on the arm
  • Repetitive overhead throwing, as in baseball and softball
  • Heavy weightlifting, especially overhead
  • Motor vehicle collisions
  • Age-related wear of the labrum, usually after age 30 to 40

How physical therapy helps

Rehabilitation is usually the first approach for most SLAP tears. A 2022 systematic review of athletes treated without surgery found that 78% of those who completed their rehab program returned to play, compared with 53.7% of all athletes studied, and return generally came in under six months in studies that reported timing. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes a therapy program of flexibility work and rotator cuff strengthening that may continue for 3 to 6 months.

Rotator cuff strengthening

Progressive exercises for the four small muscles that keep the ball centered in the socket, a core part of nonsurgical care for SLAP tears according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons.

Shoulder blade control

Strengthening and postural work for the muscles around the shoulder blade so your arm moves on a steady base, an approach the American Physical Therapy Association's ChoosePT guide describes for labral tears.

Targeted stretching

Stretches to restore lost motion and correct muscle imbalances around the shoulder, chosen based on what your exam shows.

Manual therapy

Gentle hands-on techniques to ease pain and begin restoring shoulder movement, as the ChoosePT guide describes, used alongside exercise.

Activity modification and return to sport

Guidance on which positions to limit for now, followed by a gradual progression back to lifting, throwing or overhead work.

Post-surgical rehab

If you have a repair, therapy follows your surgeon's protocol and the clinic coordinates with the surgeon. The AAOS notes strengthening typically begins 6 to 10 weeks after surgery.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist will ask how the pain started, which movements bring it on, and what your sport or job asks of your shoulder. The exam measures active and passive range of motion, rotator cuff and shoulder blade strength, and posture. Your therapist may gently place the arm in positions that reproduce your symptoms and check for signs of instability. If the findings suggest imaging would help, you will be advised to follow up with your physician.

Recovery

Recovery varies with the type of tear, your age, and how much your shoulder has to do. According to Cleveland Clinic, pain often settles in about 3 to 4 months with nonsurgical care. After a surgical repair, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes a sling for 2 to 6 weeks and, for throwing athletes, interval throwing at about 3 to 4 months. The American Physical Therapy Association says a repaired labrum may take nine to 12 months to fully heal. Some people still need surgery after a fair trial of therapy.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Limit heavy lifting and overhead reaching that bring on sharp pain, but keep moving the arm gently within a comfortable range.
  • Use a cold pack or heat for short periods to ease soreness, whichever feels better.
  • Hold off on throwing, overhead pressing and dips until you have been evaluated.
  • Notice your posture at the desk and in the car, since postural work is part of care for labral tears.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Your arm or shoulder looks deformed or out of place after an injury
  • You cannot move your arm after a fall or collision
  • Pins and needles, numbness, or a change in color or temperature in the arm or hand
  • Sudden, very bad shoulder pain, or severe pain in both shoulders
  • Shoulder pain along with fever or feeling generally unwell
  • Pain that keeps getting worse or has not improved after 2 weeks of home care

Shoulder Labral Tear (SLAP Tear): common questions

Can a SLAP tear heal without surgery?
Many SLAP tears are managed without surgery, particularly in athletes. A 2022 systematic review found that among athletes who completed a nonsurgical rehab program, 78% returned to play. Older age, a traumatic injury, a long period of symptoms and a coexisting rotator cuff injury were linked to a lower chance of success. If symptoms persist after a fair trial of therapy, a surgeon may discuss repair.
Is a SLAP tear the same as a rotator cuff tear?
No. The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles and their tendons that hold the ball of the upper arm in the socket. The labrum is a rim of cartilage around the socket itself, and a SLAP tear affects the top of that rim where the biceps tendon attaches. The two injuries can happen together, which can change how treatment is planned.
Do I need an MRI before physical therapy for a shoulder labral tear?
Not always. A physical therapist can evaluate your shoulder through your history, motion and strength testing, and positions that reproduce your symptoms. According to the American Physical Therapy Association, an MRI may be requested to confirm a labral tear. If your exam points to a significant tear, or you are not improving, your therapist will recommend following up with your physician about imaging.
Can I keep lifting weights with a SLAP tear?
Weightlifting is one of the common causes of SLAP tears, so heavy overhead and pressing work often needs to be scaled back for a while. That usually does not mean stopping all exercise. A physical therapist can show you which lifts and positions to pause, what to substitute in the meantime, and how to rebuild load gradually so you return to the gym with a plan.
What is a dead arm in baseball players?
Throwing athletes with a SLAP tear sometimes describe a 'dead arm,' a sense that the arm loses power or feels weak during or after throwing, often along with lower velocity. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists it among the symptoms of SLAP injuries in throwers. A pitcher or position player with these symptoms should be evaluated before continuing to throw.
How many physical therapy sessions does a SLAP tear take?
It depends on the tear and your goals. In a 2022 systematic review of athletes with SLAP tears, those whose nonsurgical treatment succeeded averaged 20 physical therapy sessions, while those who stopped rehab and chose surgery averaged 8. That association suggests completing the program may matter. Your therapist will set a visit plan after your evaluation and adjust it as you progress.
