SI Joint Pain

Physical therapy for sacroiliac joint pain that steadies the pelvis, eases one-sided low back and buttock pain, and helps you walk, climb stairs and sleep more comfortably.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: sacroiliac joint pain, sacroiliac joint dysfunction, SIJ dysfunction, sacroiliac pain

SI Joint Pain, explained

Your two sacroiliac (SI) joints sit at the back of the pelvis, where the sacrum at the base of the spine meets the hip bones on either side. These joints help transfer weight from your upper body to your legs and normally move only a small amount. The APTA ChoosePT guide explains that SI joint dysfunction develops when these joints become too stiff, or too loose and poorly supported.

SI joint problems are a frequent and often overlooked source of low back pain. ChoosePT reports that SI joint dysfunction is found in 10% to 25% of people who complain of low back pain and is most often diagnosed in women. Pregnancy plays a role: Mayo Clinic notes that the joints loosen and stretch for childbirth, while extra weight and a changed walking pattern add stress. Falls and arthritis are other common triggers.

Common symptoms

  • Pain on one side of the low back or pelvis
  • Aching or sharp pain in the buttock, groin or tailbone area
  • Pain that spreads down the back of the thigh, often stopping near the knee
  • Pain with running or climbing stairs
  • Discomfort after sitting, standing or walking for a long time
  • Pain that flares when you change positions

Causes and risk factors

  • A fall, car accident or other direct impact to the pelvis
  • Pregnancy and childbirth, which loosen the joint
  • Osteoarthritis or inflammatory arthritis such as ankylosing spondylitis
  • Muscle imbalances and hip problems
  • Overtraining in sports or a single sudden injury
  • Lack of exercise, which can leave muscles tight

How physical therapy helps

Exercise sits at the center of SI joint care. Because SI joint dysfunction can come from a joint that is too stiff or one that is too loose, the plan depends on what your evaluation finds. Mayo Clinic notes that a physical therapist can teach range of motion, stretching and strengthening exercises to reduce pain and protect the joints. The APTA ChoosePT guide adds manual therapy, body mechanics training and functional practice so you can return to your normal activities safely.

Pelvic stabilization exercise

Strengthening the glutes, deep abdominals and hip muscles improves control around the SI joint, which is especially useful when the joint is loose after pregnancy or injury.

Stretching and range of motion

Targeted stretches for the hips, thighs and low back reduce tension that can pull unevenly on the pelvis.

Manual therapy

Hands-on joint and soft tissue techniques can reduce pain and improve movement when the joint is stiff.

Pelvic belt guidance

Mayo Clinic notes that a physical therapist may suggest a pelvic support belt to limit motion and ease pain. Your therapist can help you judge whether one is worth trying.

Activity and body mechanics changes

You learn ways to climb stairs, get out of the car, roll in bed and lift that put less shearing load through the pelvis.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first appointment takes about an hour. Your therapist asks how the pain started, where you feel it and which activities provoke it. You may be asked to walk, climb a step and squat while your therapist watches how the pelvis moves. The exam checks spine, pelvis and hip mobility and strength, and usually includes several SI joint provocation tests, since no single test settles the question. Treatment begins that day.

Recovery

How long SI joint pain lasts depends a great deal on what set it off. Pain after a minor strain may settle sooner than pain linked to arthritis, pregnancy or an old injury, and inflammatory arthritis usually needs care from a physician alongside therapy. There is no typical timeline that fits everyone, so your therapist will set expectations after the evaluation and adjust them as your strength and symptoms change. Continuing your home exercises after discharge helps keep the pelvis stable.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Alternate ice and heat over the sore area
  • Break up long periods of sitting, standing or walking
  • Sleep on the side that does not hurt, with a pillow between your knees
  • Take stairs one step at a time, leading with the less painful leg

When to see a doctor right away

  • Pelvic or low back pain after a fall or car accident
  • Pain with fever or feeling generally unwell, which can rarely signal a joint infection
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse
  • Ongoing low back and buttock pain if you have psoriasis, Crohn's disease or another inflammatory condition

SI Joint Pain: common questions

How do I know if my pain is from my SI joint or my lower back?
It can be hard to tell on your own because the two areas overlap. SI joint pain usually sits low, on one side of the pelvis, and may spread into the buttock, groin or back of the thigh. A hands-on evaluation by a physical therapist or physician, using several SI joint provocation tests, is the most reliable way to sort it out.
Does an SI belt help SI joint pain?
It can help some people. Mayo Clinic notes a physical therapist may suggest a pelvic belt to limit motion and ease pain. A belt tends to be most useful when the joint feels loose, such as during or after pregnancy. It works best as part of an exercise program rather than as the only treatment.
Can pregnancy cause SI joint pain?
Yes. Mayo Clinic explains that the sacroiliac joints loosen and stretch to prepare for childbirth, and the added weight and changed walking pattern during pregnancy can stress them. The APTA ChoosePT guide also lists pregnancy and childbirth as risk factors for SI joint dysfunction. Gentle strengthening and positioning advice from a physical therapist, cleared by your obstetric provider, may help.
Is walking good for SI joint pain?
Moderate walking is often fine, but long walks can aggravate symptoms. Mayo Clinic lists sitting, walking or standing for a long time among activities that can worsen SI joint pain. Shorter, more frequent walks on level ground are usually easier to tolerate. If walking reliably sets off your pain, a therapist can check your gait and hip strength for contributing factors.
What is the difference between sacroiliitis and SI joint dysfunction?
Sacroiliitis means inflammation of one or both sacroiliac joints, which Mayo Clinic links to causes such as injury, osteoarthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, pregnancy and, rarely, infection. SI joint dysfunction is a broader term the APTA uses for joints that are too stiff or too loose and painful. The two overlap, and inflammatory forms may also need care from a physician.
Can SI joint pain cause pain down the leg?
It can. The APTA ChoosePT guide notes SI joint pain may spread toward the knee, and Mayo Clinic notes it can sometimes reach the groin, legs or even the feet. Pain that travels below the knee with numbness or tingling may point to a nerve root in the lower back instead, which is one reason a full evaluation matters.
