Hip Bursitis (Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome)

Physical therapy for pain on the outside of the hip, whether it comes from an irritated bursa or the gluteal tendons that attach there.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: trochanteric bursitis, greater trochanteric pain syndrome, GTPS, gluteal tendinopathy, lateral hip pain

Hip Bursitis (Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome), explained

The bony point on the outside of your hip is the greater trochanter, the top of the thigh bone. Two gluteal muscles, the gluteus medius and minimus, attach there by tendons, and small fluid-filled sacs called bursae cushion the area. For years, pain in this spot was simply called hip bursitis. Clinicians now often use the broader term greater trochanteric pain syndrome, because the pain frequently comes from the gluteal tendons themselves (gluteal tendinopathy) rather than an inflamed bursa.

The distinction matters for treatment, since tendons respond to gradual, well-dosed loading, and a program built on that idea has strong trial support. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes it is more common in women and in middle-aged and older adults, and less common in younger people and men.

Common symptoms

  • Pain at the point of the hip that can spread down the outer thigh
  • Pain lying on the affected side, often worse at night
  • Tenderness when you press on the outside of the hip
  • Pain after sitting for a long time
  • Pain with walking, climbing stairs or standing on one leg
  • Hip weakness, sometimes with a side-to-side lean or limp when walking

Causes and risk factors

  • Repetitive stress from running, stair climbing or cycling
  • Higher body weight
  • Lying on one side for long periods, or a fall onto the hip
  • A leg-length difference or spine problems such as scoliosis
  • A tight iliotibial (IT) band
  • Previous hip surgery or arthritis in the hip
  • Being female and middle-aged or older

How physical therapy helps

Education about tendon load combined with targeted exercise is the most studied therapy approach for this condition. In a randomized trial published in The BMJ in 2018, people with gluteal tendinopathy who completed 14 physiotherapist-led education and exercise sessions over eight weeks reported success more often than those who had a corticosteroid injection or took a wait-and-see approach. At one year, 78% of the education and exercise group reported success, compared with 57% of the injection group.

Load education

Understanding which daily habits and positions irritate the tendons, such as lying on the painful side or long bouts of stair climbing, and how to adjust them.

Progressive hip abductor strengthening

Exercises for the gluteus medius and minimus that start gentle and build steadily, matching how tendons adapt to load.

Walking and single-leg control

Practice with walking, single-leg standing and stairs to reduce the hip drop that adds strain to the outer hip.

Flexibility work

Stretching for the IT band and hip muscles when tightness is part of the picture, as the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes.

Activity modification

Adjusting running volume, hills and stair work while the tendons settle, then building back up in planned steps.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist will ask when the pain started, whether it wakes you at night, and how sitting, stairs and side-lying affect it. The exam includes pressing over the outer hip to find the tender spot, testing resisted hip abduction strength, and watching you stand on one leg and walk. Checking hip joint motion and the lower back helps sort out whether some of the pain is coming from the joint or the spine.

Recovery

Outer hip pain often improves with conservative care, but it takes patience. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that nonsurgical care is the usual first approach and that surgery is rarely needed. In the BMJ trial, the education and exercise program ran for eight weeks, and that group still reported better overall improvement than the injection group at one year. How quickly you improve depends on how long the pain has been present and what else is going on in the hip, so your therapist will set expectations after your evaluation.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Avoid lying on the painful side for now.
  • Break up long stretches of stair climbing, hill walking or running while symptoms are high.
  • Use an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes to ease soreness.
  • Wear comfortable shoes, and ask about a shoe insert if one leg is shorter than the other.
  • Stay active with gentle movement rather than complete rest.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Hip pain after a fall, especially if you cannot put weight on the leg
  • Fever, chills, warmth or a change in skin color around the hip or leg
  • Sudden swelling, or a hip that looks misshapen
  • Pain that is getting worse or has not improved after 2 weeks of home care
  • Pain that is regularly disturbing your sleep

Hip Bursitis (Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome): common questions

What is the difference between hip bursitis and gluteal tendinopathy?
Both cause pain on the outside of the hip. Bursitis refers to irritation of a small fluid-filled sac over the greater trochanter, while gluteal tendinopathy is irritation or degeneration of the gluteus medius and minimus tendons that attach to the same bone. Clinicians often group both under the term greater trochanteric pain syndrome, and the tendons are frequently involved.
Why does hip bursitis hurt more at night?
The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that outer hip pain from bursitis is often worse at night and when lying on the affected side. Lying on that side presses body weight directly onto the tender tendons and bursa. Rolling onto the painful hip can also wake you. Avoiding that position for a while is usually one of the first changes a therapist suggests.
Is a cortisone shot or physical therapy better for outer hip pain?
In a 2018 randomized trial in The BMJ, both an education and exercise program and a corticosteroid injection beat a wait-and-see approach at eight weeks. At one year, the education and exercise group reported success more often (78% versus 57%). At one year, pain levels were similar between those two groups, while both did better than wait and see. Your physician can discuss injection options with you.
Should I stretch my IT band for hip bursitis?
Stretching the iliotibial band is one part of the program the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes for hip bursitis, particularly when the band is tight. Most current programs, however, center on strengthening the gluteal muscles, because the tendons that attach at the outer hip are commonly involved. A therapist can check whether stretching helps or aggravates your particular symptoms.
Can hip bursitis go away on its own?
Sometimes, but it can be slow. In the 2018 BMJ trial of gluteal tendinopathy, 29% of people in the wait-and-see group reported success at eight weeks, rising to about 52% at one year, compared with about 78% of those who completed education and exercise. Waiting is an option, though a structured program tends to help more people sooner.
Does body weight affect outer hip pain?
It can. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists maintaining a healthy weight among the ways to help prevent hip bursitis, along with keeping the hip muscles strong and flexible. Lower-impact exercise that strengthens the hip without irritating the tendons can support both goals, and a therapist can help you choose activities that do not flare the outer hip.
