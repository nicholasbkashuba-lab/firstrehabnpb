Lumbar Spinal Stenosis

Physical therapy for lumbar spinal stenosis that helps you walk farther, stand longer and stay active, with exercise matched to how your back and legs respond.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: spinal stenosis, lumbar stenosis, LSS, narrowing of the spinal canal

Lumbar Spinal Stenosis, explained

Your spinal canal is the tunnel of bone that protects the nerves running down your back. Stenosis means this space has narrowed and crowds those nerves. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, discs lose water and height with age, which puts more load on the small joints of the spine. Those joints develop arthritis and bone spurs, and nearby ligaments thicken, together taking up room the nerves need.

Lumbar spinal stenosis is mainly a condition of later life. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that up to 95% of people show some degenerative changes in the spine by age 50, and that stenosis usually develops after 60, affecting men and women equally. Mayo Clinic reports most people with spinal stenosis are older than 50. Some people are born with a narrower canal and notice symptoms earlier.

Common symptoms

  • Pain, cramping or heaviness in one or both legs when you stand or walk
  • Leg symptoms that ease when you sit down or bend forward
  • Burning pain in the buttocks that spreads into the legs
  • Numbness or tingling in the buttocks, legs or feet
  • Leg weakness or a foot that catches or drags
  • Walking that feels easier while leaning on a shopping cart
  • Low back pain that may come and go

Causes and risk factors

  • Arthritis of the spinal joints and the bone spurs it produces
  • Discs that lose height and bulge with age
  • Thickened, stiff ligaments inside the spinal canal
  • A herniated disc pressing into the canal
  • Being born with a narrow spinal canal
  • A previous spine injury, such as a fracture from a car accident

How physical therapy helps

The APTA ChoosePT guide states that in most cases, symptoms of spinal stenosis can be effectively managed with physical therapy and other conservative treatments. A randomized trial led by physical therapist Anthony Delitto, published in Annals of Internal Medicine in 2015, compared a standardized physical therapy program with decompression surgery and reported similar results, according to ChoosePT. StatPearls lists stretching, strengthening and aerobic fitness among first-line conservative care, with the goal of keeping you walking and active.

Flexion-based exercise

Because bending forward tends to open space for the nerves, your therapist teaches movements and resting positions that use that posture to ease leg symptoms.

Cycling and aerobic conditioning

ChoosePT notes that bicycling may suit people with stenosis better than walking, since walking is usually more painful than sitting. Building fitness raises how long you can stay on your feet.

Trunk and leg strengthening

Stronger abdominal, back, hip and thigh muscles help support the spine and take some of the workload off the arthritic joints.

Hip and spine mobility work

Stretching tight hip flexors and improving lower back motion can make standing upright more comfortable.

Walking and posture strategies

You learn how to pace walks, use brief sitting breaks and adjust how you stand and walk to relieve pressure on the nerves.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit runs about an hour. Your therapist asks how far you can walk or how long you can stand before leg symptoms begin, and what brings relief. You may mark painful and numb areas on a body diagram. The exam includes muscle strength and sensation testing, posture and walking observation, range of motion of the spine and legs, and hands-on assessment of joint mobility. Treatment and a home program start the same day.

Recovery

Lumbar spinal stenosis is usually a long-term condition, so the aim of therapy is to manage symptoms and keep you moving. Its course varies: StatPearls notes that while some people improve over time, most see the condition gradually progress, and there is no standard recovery timeline. Many people find they can walk and stand longer once they are exercising consistently and using the positions that relieve their legs. Keeping up a home program after discharge helps protect those gains.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Sit down or lean forward when leg symptoms build during a walk, then continue
  • Try a stationary bike, or walk while leaning on a shopping cart
  • Keep moving every day in short, comfortable bouts
  • Limit long periods of standing upright and walking downhill

When to see a doctor right away

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control (seek emergency care)
  • Numbness in the genital or saddle area
  • Leg weakness that is quickly getting worse
  • Sudden inability to walk
  • New or rapidly worsening numbness in the legs

Lumbar Spinal Stenosis: common questions

Can spinal stenosis be treated without surgery?
Usually, yes. The APTA ChoosePT guide states that in most cases spinal stenosis symptoms can be effectively managed with physical therapy and other conservative treatments, and that only the most severe cases need surgery or spinal injections. Conservative care focuses on exercise, positions that relieve pressure on the nerves and building walking tolerance. Your physician decides with you if surgery becomes necessary.
Why does spinal stenosis hurt more when I walk?
Standing and walking upright arch the lower back slightly, which narrows the space around the nerves further. StatPearls describes this pattern, called neurogenic claudication: pain worsens with standing, walking and leaning back, and improves with sitting or bending forward. It also notes many people tolerate walking uphill better than downhill for the same reason.
What exercises should I avoid with spinal stenosis?
Movements that arch the lower back for long periods tend to aggravate symptoms. StatPearls advises avoiding downhill walking and excessive backward bending. That does not mean avoiding exercise. The APTA ChoosePT guide notes that some exercises suit stenosis better than others, and a physical therapist can show you which ones to use and which to hold off on.
Is physical therapy as effective as surgery for spinal stenosis?
For some patients, research suggests it can be comparable. According to the APTA ChoosePT summary, a 2015 randomized trial in Annals of Internal Medicine compared standardized physical therapy with decompression surgery and found similar results, while surgery carried a higher complication risk. An accompanying editorial suggested patients be offered a rigorous physical therapy program. Every case differs, so discuss options with your physician.
Does spinal stenosis get worse over time?
It can. StatPearls notes that while some people improve with time, the majority see gradual progression. Because the narrowing is tied to age-related changes in the spine, staying strong and active is a reasonable way to help protect your walking ability, and ChoosePT notes regular exercise can help limit its impact. Rapidly worsening weakness or bladder changes need prompt medical attention.
Is a stationary bike good for spinal stenosis?
For many people it is a good fit. Sitting on a bike keeps the spine slightly bent forward, which tends to give the nerves more room. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons mentions that some people manage symptoms by using a stationary bike, and the ChoosePT guide notes bicycling may be easier than walking. Start with short, easy sessions.
