Sciatica

Physical therapy for sciatica that calms the irritated nerve, eases pain running down your leg, and rebuilds the strength you need to sit, walk and work again.

Also called: lumbar radiculopathy, pinched nerve in the lower back, sciatic nerve pain

Sciatica, explained

Sciatica describes pain that follows the path of the sciatic nerve, the largest nerve in your body. It is formed by nerve roots that leave the lower spine (from L4 down to S3), then travels through the buttock and down the back of the leg. When one of those roots is pressed on or irritated, you can feel symptoms anywhere along that route, often well below the spot where the problem starts.

Sciatica is common. According to StatPearls, a clinical reference from the National Library of Medicine, between 10% and 40% of people have it at some point in their lives. Cleveland Clinic notes it rarely happens before age 20 unless related to an injury, and the APTA ChoosePT guide says it most often affects people between 30 and 50. Mayo Clinic lists long hours of driving, heavy lifting and prolonged sitting among the risks.

Common symptoms

  • Pain that travels from the low back or buttock down the back of one leg
  • Burning, sharp or electric-shock feelings in the leg
  • Numbness or tingling in the leg, foot or toes
  • Weakness in the leg or foot
  • Pain that flares with sitting, coughing, sneezing or bending forward
  • Trouble standing up straight or getting up from a chair
  • Symptoms that usually affect one side of the body

Causes and risk factors

  • A herniated or bulging disc pressing on a nerve root
  • Lumbar spinal stenosis, a narrowing of the spaces around the nerves
  • Bone spurs from arthritis in the spine
  • Work that involves heavy lifting, twisting or long hours of driving
  • Long periods of sitting and low physical activity
  • Excess body weight, which adds load to the spine
  • Diabetes, which raises the risk of nerve damage

How physical therapy helps

Most sciatica gets better without an operation. The APTA ChoosePT guide states that the majority of lumbar radiculopathy and sciatica cases recover without surgery and respond well to physical therapy. The 2021 low back pain clinical practice guideline in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy supports trunk strengthening and muscle activation exercise for back pain with leg pain, and lists nerve mobilization and joint mobilization as options when symptoms have lasted longer. Treatment first settles the nerve, then builds back strength and tolerance.

Directional preference movements

Your therapist tests repeated bending or arching movements to find a direction that draws leg pain back toward your spine, a sign called centralization, then turns it into a short home routine.

Nerve mobilization

Gentle sliding movements of the leg and spine help the irritated nerve glide more freely. The 2021 JOSPT guideline supports this alongside other treatment for longer-lasting back pain with leg pain.

Trunk strengthening and activation

Exercises for the deep abdominal and back muscles build support around the irritated level. The same guideline supports this approach for back pain that travels into the leg.

Hands-on joint mobilization

Skilled manual techniques to the lower back can reduce pain and stiffness so that exercise feels more manageable.

Sitting, lifting and posture coaching

You learn ways to sit, bend and lift that put less strain on the nerve root during work, driving and daily chores.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour. Your therapist asks exactly where the leg symptoms travel, what sets them off and what settles them. The exam checks how your lower back moves, leg strength, skin sensation and reflexes, and how the nerve tolerates a straight leg raise. Repeated movement testing shows whether your symptoms shift toward the spine. Treatment starts that same day, and you leave with a home program.

Recovery

Recovery time differs from person to person. StatPearls reports that most cases of sciatica resolve in less than 4 to 6 weeks, and Cleveland Clinic notes that many people improve within four to six weeks even without medical treatment. The APTA ChoosePT guide says improvement may be achieved in 4 to 8 weeks when a program of posture, pain reduction, stretching and strengthening is followed. Symptoms that have already lasted for months usually take longer to settle, and flare-ups can return, which is why keeping up your home exercises matters after discharge.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Stay gently active with short walks instead of several days of bed rest
  • Try ice or heat on your low back, whichever eases your pain more
  • Break up long stretches of sitting by standing and moving around
  • Put a small pillow or rolled towel behind your low back when you sit
  • Hold off on heavy lifting and twisting until you have been evaluated

When to see a doctor right away

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or trouble passing urine (go to the emergency room)
  • Numbness in the groin, genitals or inner thighs
  • Sudden numbness or weakness in a leg
  • Leg weakness that keeps getting worse
  • Pain that started after a violent injury such as a car crash or bad fall
  • Back and leg pain with fever or a history of cancer

Sciatica: common questions

Can sciatica go away on its own?
Often, yes. StatPearls, a clinical reference from the National Library of Medicine, reports that most cases of sciatica resolve in less than 4 to 6 weeks without long-term problems. Some people have symptoms that linger or come back, especially with long hours of sitting or lifting. If leg pain is not easing after a week or two, or keeps returning, a physical therapy evaluation can find what is driving it.
What is the difference between sciatica and a herniated disc?
Sciatica is a description of symptoms: pain, tingling or numbness that travels along the sciatic nerve into the leg. A herniated disc is one possible cause, where the soft center of a spinal disc pushes out and presses on a nerve root. Lumbar spinal stenosis and bone spurs can also cause sciatica, so treatment depends on the source.
Is walking good for sciatica?
For many people, short and comfortable walks help. The 2021 JOSPT low back pain guideline supports teaching people to remain active, and Mayo Clinic lists regular exercise as a way to protect the back. Keep walks at a pace and distance that does not push pain further down your leg. If walking makes leg symptoms spread, mention it to your physical therapist.
Why does sciatica hurt more when I sit?
Sitting, especially slumped, bends the lower spine forward. StatPearls notes that sciatica pain tends to increase with lumbar flexion, bending and coughing, and the ChoosePT guide lists sitting among common triggers. Sitting with support behind your low back, getting up often and avoiding deep soft couches can help. A therapist can show you positions that take pressure off the nerve.
Do I need an MRI before physical therapy for sciatica?
Not usually. Most people with sciatica are evaluated and treated based on their symptoms and a physical exam. The 2021 JOSPT low back pain guideline notes that high-quality back pain guidelines consistently recommend using imaging selectively, and screening for warning signs of a more serious problem. If your therapist finds signs such as significant or worsening weakness, they will refer you back to your physician.
Can I see a physical therapist for sciatica without a referral?
The APTA ChoosePT guide notes that you can contact a physical therapist directly for an evaluation. Whether your plan needs a physician referral or prescription before therapy is covered can vary.
