Patellar Tendinitis (Jumper's Knee)

Patellar tendinitis causes pain just below the kneecap that builds with jumping and running, and gradually loading the tendon with the right exercises is the core of treatment.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: jumper's knee, patellar tendinopathy, patellar tendonitis, patellar tendon pain

Patellar Tendinitis (Jumper's Knee), explained

Your patellar tendon connects the bottom of your kneecap to your shinbone. It works with your thigh muscles to straighten the knee, absorb landings and push off when you jump. Mayo Clinic explains that repeated stress causes tiny tears in the tendon, and when it does not get enough time to recover, the body tries to heal by thickening it. Clinicians often call the condition patellar tendinopathy for this reason.

A 2023 review in Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery describes it as an overuse injury that affects both professional and recreational athletes, most often in jumping sports. Pain usually builds gradually rather than starting with one injury. Mayo Clinic lists frequent running and jumping, tight thigh muscles, muscle imbalances and certain chronic illnesses as risk factors, and notes it is more common in men.

Common symptoms

  • Pain between the bottom of the kneecap and the top of the shinbone
  • Pain that first shows up during jumping or after an intense workout
  • Pain that builds gradually over weeks or months
  • Pain climbing stairs as the condition progresses
  • Pain getting up from a chair
  • Pain that limits how well you can play or train

Causes and risk factors

  • Repeated jumping, landing and running
  • Ramping up training without enough recovery time
  • Tight quadriceps and hamstring muscles
  • Strength imbalances between leg muscles
  • Certain chronic illnesses, such as kidney failure, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or diabetes
  • Male sex

How physical therapy helps

Exercise that loads the tendon is the foundation of care. A 2021 randomized trial in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Breda and colleagues) found that a staged, progressive tendon-loading program improved pain and function more than eccentric exercise after 24 weeks, and the authors recommend it as the first conservative treatment. Mayo Clinic adds stretching, gradual strengthening and patellar strapping, and cautions that working through pain can further damage the tendon. Therapy organizes all of this into a plan you can follow.

Progressive tendon loading

A staged program like the one tested in the BJSM trial: steady holds first, then slow strengthening, then springier jumping loads and finally sport drills, advancing only when pain stays mild.

Quadriceps and hip strengthening

Mayo Clinic notes that weak thigh muscles strain the patellar tendon, so building strength around the knee and hip helps share the load.

Flexibility work

Regular stretching of the quadriceps and hamstrings, since Mayo Clinic lists tightness in these muscles as a risk factor.

Strapping or taping

A patellar strap or tape may take pressure off the tendon and ease pain during activity, according to Mayo Clinic.

Training load and return to sport

Adjusting jumping volume and practice schedules, then rebuilding to full sport with planned recovery days between hard sessions.

What to expect at your first visit

We ask about your sport, training load and when in your activity the pain starts. Your therapist presses along the tendon to find the sore spot, measures quadriceps and hip strength, and checks quadriceps and hamstring flexibility. You may perform a single-leg squat or small hops while rating your pain. The BJSM trial used a single-leg squat pain rating to decide when to progress, and we use a similar baseline to guide your loading program.

Recovery

Patellar tendinopathy usually takes patience. The 2023 review notes that it often persists for years in athletes, which is why a structured plan matters. In the BJSM trial, outcomes were measured after 24 weeks of exercise therapy, and participants with long-standing symptoms (a median of two years) improved meaningfully, though fewer than half had fully returned to sport by then. Mayo Clinic notes that surgery is rarely needed. Expect gradual gains with occasional flare-ups that guide how we adjust load.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Cut back on the jumping and running that bring on pain instead of pushing through it
  • Ice the tendon after activities that aggravate it
  • Try a patellar strap during activity if it eases your pain
  • Stay active with lower-impact exercise that does not provoke the tendon
  • Stretch your quadriceps and hamstrings gently and regularly

When to see a doctor right away

  • Knee pain that continues or gets worse despite rest (Mayo Clinic)
  • Pain that keeps you from doing your daily activities
  • Swelling or redness around the knee joint
  • A sudden pop or sharp pain below the kneecap during activity, since Mayo Clinic notes the tendon can rarely rupture
  • Fever along with a red, warm, swollen knee

Patellar Tendinitis (Jumper's Knee): common questions

What is the difference between patellar tendinitis and patellar tendinopathy?
They describe the same painful patellar tendon, often called jumper's knee. Tendinitis suggests inflammation, while tendinopathy is a broader term for a tendon that is painful and not handling load well. Mayo Clinic explains that repeated stress causes small tears and the tendon thickens as it tries to heal, and much of the research now uses the term patellar tendinopathy.
Should I stop jumping if I have jumper's knee?
You usually need to reduce jumping rather than stop all activity. Mayo Clinic advises avoiding activities that cause or worsen the pain, since working through pain can damage the tendon further. In the 2021 BJSM trial, jumping and sport drills were reintroduced in stages, only after pain during a single-leg squat stayed at 3 or lower on a 10-point scale.
Do patellar tendon straps work?
They can help some people. Mayo Clinic states that a patellar strap or taping can take pressure off the patellar tendon and may relieve pain. A strap does not rebuild the tendon on its own, so it works best as a short-term aid during activity while you follow a progressive strengthening program that improves how the tendon handles load.
How long does jumper's knee take to heal?
Often longer than people expect. A 2023 review in Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery notes that patellar tendinopathy often persists for years. In a 2021 randomized trial, people with long-standing symptoms were assessed after 24 weeks of exercise therapy, and many had improved substantially by then. Recent, milder cases may settle sooner, especially when training load is adjusted early.
Are eccentric exercises good for patellar tendinitis?
Eccentric exercise, such as slow lowering on a decline board, has long been used and can help. However, a 2021 randomized trial in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that a progressive tendon-loading program, moving from holds to strength work to jumping and sport drills, gave better results at 24 weeks than eccentric exercise alone, and the authors recommend it as the initial treatment.
Does patellar tendinitis need surgery?
Rarely. Mayo Clinic notes that surgery is seldom needed for patellar tendinitis, and a 2023 review describes most treatments as non-operative, with surgery considered only after conservative care fails. The same review notes there is no clear evidence on which surgical approach works best. For most people, a well-progressed exercise program is the place to start.
