Physical Therapy After Hip Replacement

Physical therapy after hip replacement that respects your surgeon's precautions while you rebuild hip strength, balance and a steady walk.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: total hip replacement rehab, total hip arthroplasty rehabilitation, THA physical therapy, THR rehab

About the surgery, and why rehab matters

In a total hip replacement, the surgeon removes the damaged ball at the top of the thighbone and replaces it with a metal stem topped by a metal or ceramic ball. The worn socket in the pelvis is lined with a metal shell and a plastic, ceramic or metal liner. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, citing federal data, reports that more than 450,000 of these surgeries are done each year in the United States.

According to the AAOS, most people who have a hip replacement are between 50 and 80. The new joint addresses the painful surfaces, but the hip muscles that were weakened by years of arthritis and by the surgery itself need retraining. Rehab helps you walk without a limp, protect the new joint while it settles in, and return to the low impact activities you enjoy, all within the protocol your surgeon sets.

What rehab works on

  • Weak hip muscles, especially on the side of the buttock
  • Limping or leaning to one side when walking
  • Stiffness in the hip and thigh
  • Swelling in the thigh, knee or ankle
  • Reliance on a walker, crutches or cane
  • Trouble with stairs, low chairs, and getting in and out of a car
  • Unsteady balance and worry about the new hip slipping

Why people have this surgery

  • Osteoarthritis, the most common reason for hip replacement
  • Rheumatoid arthritis and related inflammatory joint diseases
  • Posttraumatic arthritis after a hip injury or fracture
  • Osteonecrosis, when poor blood supply causes the femoral head to collapse
  • Childhood hip disease that leads to arthritis later in life
  • Hip pain and stiffness that limit daily activities or persist at rest despite medicines and therapy

How physical therapy helps

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes exercise as a key part of recovery at home, including a graduated walking program and specific exercises done several times a day to restore movement and strength. A 2022 systematic review in the American Journal of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation found low-strength evidence that different rehabilitation programs after hip replacement may give similar results for pain, strength and daily activities. In practice, following the protocol your surgeon prescribes, done consistently and progressed safely, is a sound approach.

Hip strengthening

Progressive exercises for the muscles that hold your pelvis level when you stand on one leg, which helps reduce limping.

Gait training

Practice walking with your walker, crutches or cane, then stepping down to less support when your surgeon and therapist agree it is safe.

Precaution coaching

Learning to sit, stand, dress, bathe and get in and out of bed or a car while following the hip precautions your surgeon assigns.

Balance training

Standing and stepping drills that build confidence and lower the chance of a fall, which can damage a new hip.

Graduated walking program

A walking plan that starts indoors and progresses outside as your endurance improves, as the AAOS recommends.

Home exercise program

Exercises you repeat several times a day between visits, updated as you get stronger.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour and includes treatment the same day. Your therapist reviews your surgeon's protocol, your weight bearing status and any hip precautions you were given. They check hip motion within your allowed limits, test strength in the hip and thigh, and watch how you walk, turn, sit and stand. They also look at balance and how you manage stairs. You leave with a home program that fits your precautions.

Recovery

Every recovery is different, and your surgeon sets the timeline. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says most people can resume light daily activities within 3 to 6 weeks after a hip replacement. MedlinePlus notes that most people no longer need a walker or crutches after 2 to 4 weeks and that you should have someone with you at home for the first 1 to 2 weeks. MedlinePlus also advises extra care to avoid dislocating the new hip, especially in the first few months. Health, strength before surgery and the surgical approach all affect pace.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Follow every hip precaution your surgeon gave you, since rules can differ by surgical approach.
  • Avoid low chairs and soft sofas, and use a firm chair with arms, as MedlinePlus advises.
  • Get up and walk at least every 45 minutes instead of sitting for long periods.
  • Use your walker or crutches until your surgeon says you can stop.
  • Keep walkways clear of rugs and cords, and carry small items in a bag on your walker so your hands stay free.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Your hip feels like it slipped out of place, or you suddenly cannot move or bear weight on the leg
  • Pain or swelling in your calf or leg, especially if the leg is red and warmer than the other, which can signal a blood clot
  • Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain (call 911)
  • Fever above 101°F, chills, or redness, drainage or increasing pain at the incision
  • A fall or injury to the leg that had surgery
  • A sudden increase in hip pain or pain your medicine does not control

Physical Therapy After Hip Replacement: common questions

What are hip precautions after hip replacement?
Hip precautions are movement rules that lower the risk of dislocating a new hip. MedlinePlus lists common ones: do not cross your legs, avoid bending the hip past 90 degrees, do not bend far forward at the waist, and do not sleep on your stomach or the operated side. Surgeons assign precautions based on how the surgery was done, so follow your own surgeon's list.
How long will I need a walker after hip replacement?
MedlinePlus says most people stop using a walker or crutches 2 to 4 weeks after hip replacement surgery. The exact timing depends on your weight bearing instructions, strength and balance. Check with your surgeon before you stop using your walking aid. Your physical therapist can help you move from a walker to a cane and then to walking on your own.
Why do I still limp after my hip replacement?
A limp after hip replacement often comes from weak hip muscles, especially those on the side of the buttock that keep your pelvis level as you step. Pain, stiffness and habits formed before surgery can add to it. Strengthening and gait training in physical therapy target these causes. Tell your surgeon about a limp that is getting worse rather than better.
Does it matter which rehab program I do after hip replacement?
A 2022 systematic review in the American Journal of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation compared different rehabilitation programs after hip replacement and found they did not differ much for pain, strength, daily activities or quality of life. The authors rated the evidence as low and called for more research. Following your surgeon's protocol consistently is the practical takeaway.
What activities can I do after a hip replacement?
According to MedlinePlus, most people can eventually return to low impact activities such as hiking, gardening, swimming, tennis and golf. Downhill skiing and contact sports such as football and soccer are usually off the list. Your surgeon decides when you are cleared for each activity, and your therapist can help you build the strength to get there.
How common are complications after hip replacement?
The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons reports that serious complications such as joint infection occur in fewer than 2 percent of hip replacement patients. Blood clots and dislocation are other known risks. Knowing the warning signs, such as calf swelling, fever, or a feeling that the hip has slipped, helps you get care quickly if a problem appears.
