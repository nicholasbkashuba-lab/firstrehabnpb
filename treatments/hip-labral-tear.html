Hip Labral Tear

Physical therapy for a torn hip labrum that builds strength and better movement around the joint to ease groin pain and catching.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: torn hip labrum, acetabular labral tear, hip labrum tear

Hip Labral Tear, explained

The labrum is a ring of cartilage that lines the rim of your hip socket. It deepens the socket, helps keep the ball of the thigh bone seated, and adds stability and shock absorption to the joint. A labral tear is a fray or split in that ring. According to Mayo Clinic, many hip labral tears cause no symptoms at all, while others cause deep groin pain and a catching sensation with certain movements.

A common contributor is femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), where extra bone on the ball or socket pinches the labrum during deep bending and turning. Cleveland Clinic calls FAI the most common cause, and our blog post on hip impingement covers it in more depth. Labral tears are also linked to a shallow socket (dysplasia), injuries, and sports with twisting, pivoting or extreme hip motion, such as hockey, soccer, golf, dance and long-distance running.

Common symptoms

  • A deep ache in the front of the hip or groin
  • Clicking, locking or catching in the hip
  • Sharp pain when squatting, pivoting or bringing the knee toward the chest
  • Pain that builds with long periods of sitting, standing or walking
  • Stiffness or limited hip motion
  • A sense that the hip is weak or unstable

Causes and risk factors

  • Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI)
  • Hip dysplasia, a shallow hip socket
  • Trauma such as a car accident, fall or hip dislocation
  • Sports with repeated twisting, cutting or pivoting
  • Activities that demand extreme hip range, such as ballet
  • Hip osteoarthritis

How physical therapy helps

Cleveland Clinic notes that surgery is the only way to repair the torn tissue itself, so the goal of therapy is less pain and better function, reached by changing how the hip is loaded. The 2023 APTA clinical practice guideline for nonarthritic hip pain gives its strongest recommendation to multimodal care for impingement and labral injuries: activity modification and strengthening of the hip, trunk and leg muscles, combined with manual therapy, movement correction, stretching and balance work.

Hip and trunk strengthening

Progressive exercise for the gluteal muscles, hip rotators, iliopsoas and core, the muscle groups the APTA guideline names for labral injuries.

Movement pattern training

Practice with squats, stairs, sit-to-stand and sport movements to reduce pinching positions and share load more evenly around the hip.

Activity modification

Temporarily limiting deep hip bending, prolonged sitting and pivoting drills that provoke catching.

Manual therapy

Joint and soft tissue mobilization when stiffness or muscle tension is limiting hip motion.

Balance and neuromuscular training

Single-leg control and balance drills that prepare the hip for running, cutting and return to sport.

Post-surgical rehab

After hip arthroscopy, care follows your surgeon's protocol and the clinic coordinates with the surgeon throughout recovery.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist will ask where the pain sits (groin, side or buttock), which movements trigger catching, and how long you can sit or walk. The exam measures hip range of motion and strength, then uses provocation tests such as FADIR (flexion, adduction, internal rotation) and FABER to see which positions reproduce your pain. Watching a squat, single-leg squat and step-down shows how your hip, pelvis and knee move together.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the size of the tear, whether FAI or dysplasia is driving it, and your activity goals. Many people start with a structured nonsurgical program. Mayo Clinic advises seeing a physician if symptoms worsen or have not improved within six weeks. If surgery is needed, Cleveland Clinic puts typical recovery at around four to six months. Mayo Clinic also notes that a labral tear can make osteoarthritis in that joint more likely, so persistent symptoms deserve attention rather than months of pushing through.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Avoid positions that bring on sharp pain or catching, often deep squats and twisting on a planted leg.
  • Break up long periods of sitting, standing or walking with short changes of position.
  • Use an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes during flare-ups.
  • Stay active within comfort with lower-impact activity instead of stopping altogether.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Severe hip pain after a fall, car accident or sports collision
  • You cannot put weight on the leg or move the hip
  • The hip looks misshapen or the leg appears shorter than usual
  • Fever, chills, or swelling and warmth around the hip
  • Symptoms that get worse or do not improve within six weeks

Hip Labral Tear: common questions

Can a hip labral tear heal on its own?
Cleveland Clinic states that a torn hip labrum does not heal by itself and that surgery is the only way to repair it. That does not mean every tear needs surgery. Mayo Clinic notes that many labral tears cause no symptoms, and for painful tears, the APTA guideline supports a program of strengthening, movement training and activity changes to reduce pain and improve function.
What is the difference between FAI and a hip labral tear?
Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is a bone shape problem: extra bone on the ball or the rim of the socket causes the two to pinch together during deep bending and rotation. A labral tear is damage to the cartilage ring around the socket. FAI is one of the most common reasons the labrum tears, so the two often occur together and are treated together.
Can I avoid surgery for a hip labral tear?
Many people try nonsurgical care first, and the 2023 APTA guideline gives its strongest recommendation to a combined program of strengthening, activity modification, manual therapy, movement correction and balance work for labral injuries. Some people improve enough to stay active without surgery, while others continue to have catching and pain and choose hip arthroscopy after talking with an orthopedic surgeon.
Is it OK to run with a hip labral tear?
It depends on your symptoms. Mayo Clinic lists long-distance running among the sports linked to labral tears, and repeated hip motion can aggravate an irritated labrum. If running brings on sharp pain or catching, it usually needs to be scaled back for a while. APTA's ChoosePT guide describes moving back into more demanding activities once your pain, strength and motion improve.
How is a hip labral tear diagnosed?
Diagnosis usually combines symptoms, a physical exam and imaging. A 2025 consensus statement from Canadian sports medicine physicians and surgeons agreed that front-of-groin pain, pain with deep hip bending and sharp catching with rotation make a labral tear more likely, and recommended X-rays of the pelvis and hip for anyone with a suspected tear. A physician orders and reviews imaging.
How long is recovery after hip arthroscopy for a labral tear?
Cleveland Clinic reports that recovery from hip labral tear surgery typically takes around four to six months. Rehabilitation follows the surgeon's protocol, which may include early limits on weight bearing and motion before strengthening and return to sport. A 2025 consensus statement also advises waiting at least 6 months after surgery before investigating persistent symptoms further.
