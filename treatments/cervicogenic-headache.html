Head & Neck

Cervicogenic Headache

Physical therapy for headaches that start in the neck, using hands-on care and targeted exercise to restore neck motion and reduce how often headaches return.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: neck headache, headache from the neck, CGH, neck-related headache

What is Cervicogenic Headache?

A cervicogenic headache is head pain that comes from a problem in your neck. Cleveland Clinic describes it as referred pain: you feel it in your head, but the source is in the bones, joints or soft tissues of the upper neck. StatPearls explains that nerves from the top three neck levels (C1 to C3) share a relay point in the brainstem with nerves from the face and head, so the brain can read neck pain as headache.

Cervicogenic headaches are less common than many other headache types. Cleveland Clinic reports that they affect between 0.4% and 4% of people who have headaches. Symptoms usually begin after age 30, though Cleveland Clinic notes many people do not seek care until their late 40s. Whiplash, arthritis and other neck injuries are common starting points.

Common symptoms

  • Headache on one side that starts at the base of the skull or in the neck
  • Pain that spreads toward the temple, forehead or behind the eye
  • Reduced neck movement, especially turning the head
  • Headaches set off or worsened by neck movement or certain neck positions
  • Tender muscles at the back of the head on the painful side
  • Neck pain alongside the headache, though not in every case

Causes and risk factors

  • Whiplash or another neck injury
  • Arthritis in the joints of the upper neck
  • A herniated or slipped disc in the neck
  • A pinched nerve in the neck
  • Neck muscle sprains
  • Long periods of slouched or forward-head posture

How physical therapy helps

Cleveland Clinic notes that many people find success managing these headaches with physical therapy. The 2017 neck pain clinical practice guideline in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy recommends that, for chronic neck pain with headache, clinicians combine cervical or cervicothoracic mobilization or manipulation with stretching, strengthening and endurance exercise for the neck and shoulder girdle. Treatment targets the neck problem that is feeding the headache.

Upper neck joint mobilization

Gentle, skilled hands-on techniques to the stiff upper neck joints aim to restore motion and reduce the pain signal that refers into the head.

Self-mobilization exercise

The JOSPT guideline supports a C1 to C2 self-SNAG, a towel-assisted rotation exercise you can repeat at home to keep the top of the neck moving.

Neck strength and endurance training

Exercises for the deep neck muscles build the endurance needed to hold your head comfortably through a workday.

Shoulder girdle exercise

Stretching and strengthening the upper back and shoulder blade muscles, as the guideline recommends, reduces strain on the neck.

Posture and workstation changes

Your therapist reviews how you sit, drive and use screens, and suggests adjustments that limit the positions that bring on your headaches.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour. Your therapist asks about your headache pattern, where the pain starts, what neck positions or activities set it off, and screens for warning signs that point to another cause. The 2017 JOSPT guideline recommends measuring active neck range of motion, performing the cervical flexion-rotation test and checking upper neck joint mobility. Posture and shoulder blade strength are assessed as well. Treatment and a home program begin that day.

Recovery

Response to treatment varies. Cleveland Clinic notes that headaches can feel worse when physical therapy begins, and that your therapist paces exercise to limit this. Cleveland Clinic also cites one study in which 72% of people had at least 50% fewer headaches 12 months after starting physical therapy. Results like these are not guaranteed, and long-standing headaches or older injuries may take more time, so consistent home exercise is part of the plan.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Set up your desk and screen so you are not craning your head forward
  • Use a pillow that supports the natural curve of your neck
  • Take short breaks to move your neck and shoulders during long tasks
  • Stay regularly active with exercise you enjoy
  • Keep a simple log of neck positions or activities that come before each headache

When to see a doctor right away

  • A sudden, explosive or violent headache (call 911)
  • A headache that feels like the worst of your life
  • Headache with trouble speaking, vision changes, confusion, loss of balance, or weakness in an arm or leg
  • Headache with fever, a stiff neck, nausea and vomiting
  • A headache that follows a head injury
  • New headaches that begin after age 50

Cervicogenic Headache: common questions

How do I know if my headache is coming from my neck?
Cervicogenic headaches usually affect one side, start at the base of the skull or in the neck, and spread forward toward the temple or eye. Cleveland Clinic notes they often come with limited neck movement and get worse when you move your neck. A physical exam that tests neck motion and the upper neck joints helps confirm whether the neck is the source.
What is the difference between a cervicogenic headache and a migraine?
A migraine is a primary headache, meaning it is a condition of its own. Cleveland Clinic classifies cervicogenic headache as a secondary headache, caused by an underlying problem in the neck. Cervicogenic headaches tend to stay on one side, follow neck movement or posture and come with reduced neck motion. The two can overlap, so a physician should evaluate unclear or changing headache patterns.
Can poor posture cause cervicogenic headaches?
Posture can contribute. The APTA ChoosePT headache guide lists poor posture, fatigue and stress among factors in neck-related headaches, and Cleveland Clinic recommends good posture, avoiding slouching and using a supportive pillow as prevention. Sustained forward-head positions load the upper neck joints and muscles. Posture changes work best combined with neck and shoulder exercise.
Can a physical therapist help headaches caused by the neck?
Often, yes. Cleveland Clinic notes that many people find success managing cervicogenic headaches with physical therapy. The 2017 JOSPT neck pain guideline recommends joint mobilization or manipulation combined with neck and shoulder girdle stretching, strengthening and endurance exercise for chronic neck pain with headache. A physical therapist evaluates your neck and builds a plan around those findings.
Can whiplash cause cervicogenic headaches?
It can. Cleveland Clinic lists whiplash among the injuries and conditions of the cervical spine that can lead to cervicogenic headache. Any headache after a car accident or blow to the head should first be checked by a physician to rule out other injuries before neck rehabilitation begins.
Do cervicogenic headaches show up on an MRI?
Not always. Cleveland Clinic explains that X-rays, CT scans or MRIs can identify structural neck problems, but a normal scan does not rule out the neck as the source of your headache. Diagnosis relies heavily on your history and physical exam, matched against criteria from the International Classification of Headache Disorders.
