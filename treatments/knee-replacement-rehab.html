Physical Therapy After Knee Replacement

One-on-one physical therapy after knee replacement that follows your surgeon's protocol and helps you get your knee bending, straightening and carrying you again.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: total knee replacement rehab, total knee arthroplasty rehabilitation, TKA physical therapy, TKR rehab

About the surgery, and why rehab matters

A total knee replacement resurfaces the worn ends of your thighbone and shinbone with metal parts, places a plastic spacer between them so they glide, and sometimes resurfaces the underside of the kneecap. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes it as one of the most successful procedures in medicine. The new joint removes the arthritic surfaces, but your muscles, scar tissue and walking habits still need retraining after surgery.

Most people who have this surgery are between 50 and 80, according to the AAOS, although surgeons decide based on pain and function rather than age. Rehabilitation matters because the knee tends to stiffen and the thigh muscles weaken quickly after the operation. Therapy starts in the hospital, and an outpatient program then follows your surgeon's protocol to restore bending, straightening, strength and confident walking.

What rehab works on

  • Stiffness, especially trouble fully straightening or bending the knee
  • Swelling around the knee, calf and ankle
  • Weak thigh muscles that make the leg feel unreliable
  • Pain with activity and at night in the first weeks
  • Limping or relying on a walker or cane
  • Difficulty with stairs and getting in and out of chairs or a car
  • Unsteady balance and worry about falling

Why people have this surgery

  • Osteoarthritis, the wear and tear type that softens and wears away knee cartilage
  • Rheumatoid arthritis or other inflammatory arthritis
  • Posttraumatic arthritis after a knee fracture or ligament injury
  • Severe knee pain that limits walking, stairs or rising from a chair
  • Pain at rest, day or night
  • Bowing in or out of the knee from advanced arthritis
  • Not enough improvement from medicines, injections or earlier physical therapy

How physical therapy helps

The American Physical Therapy Association's clinical practice guideline on total knee arthroplasty recommends supervised physical therapy after surgery and gives its strongest rating to motor function training, meaning practice with balance, walking and moving both legs evenly. The same guideline supports high-intensity, progressive strength training started early in recovery to improve function, strength and motion, and teaching you to use ice for early pain. Your therapist follows your surgeon's protocol and adjusts the pace to how your knee responds.

Range of motion work

Passive, assisted and active exercises to regain knee straightening and bending. The AAOS notes that limited motion usually improves with physical therapy and range of motion exercises.

Progressive strengthening

Quadriceps, hip and calf exercises that start early and get harder as you heal, an approach the APTA guideline supports for function, strength and motion.

Gait and balance training

Practice walking with an even stride, weaning from a walker or cane when your surgeon and therapist agree, and balance drills to lower fall risk.

Functional practice

Rehearsing stairs, sit to stand, and car transfers so daily tasks feel safe again.

Swelling and pain education

Guidance on ice, elevation and pacing activity, including how to use ice before and after exercise.

Home exercise program

A written plan you do daily between visits, updated as your knee progresses.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit takes about an hour and includes treatment the same day. Your therapist reviews your surgeon's protocol and any restrictions, checks the incision area for swelling, and measures how far your knee bends and straightens. They test thigh strength, watch you walk and climb a step, and may time tests such as the 30 second sit to stand or the Timed Up and Go, which the APTA guideline suggests for tracking progress. You leave with a home program.

Recovery

Recovery after knee replacement is gradual and differs from person to person. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, most people resume normal daily activities within 3 to 6 weeks and return to driving at about 4 to 6 weeks, once the knee bends enough and reaction time is adequate. MedlinePlus notes some people use a walker or crutches for up to 3 months. The AAOS adds that full motion is uncommon, though most people can nearly straighten the knee and bend it enough for stairs and getting into a car. Your surgeon decides when restrictions lift.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Do the exercises your hospital therapist gave you every day until your outpatient therapy begins.
  • Get up and move at least every 45 minutes, which MedlinePlus recommends during recovery.
  • Ice your knee before and after exercise to help with swelling, as MedlinePlus advises.
  • When lying down, rest a pillow under your heel or ankle, not behind your knee, to help keep the knee straight.
  • Clear loose rugs and cords and use your walker or cane as instructed to avoid falls.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Increasing pain, tenderness or swelling in your calf, ankle or foot, which can signal a blood clot
  • Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, which can mean a clot has reached the lungs (call 911)
  • Fever above 101°F, chills, or redness, drainage or worsening swelling at the incision
  • Bleeding that soaks through the dressing and does not stop with pressure
  • Foot or toes that turn darker than normal or feel cool to the touch
  • Pain that is not relieved by your pain medicine or keeps getting worse with activity and rest

Physical Therapy After Knee Replacement: common questions

How soon after knee replacement should outpatient physical therapy start?
An expert panel published in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy recommended that outpatient rehabilitation begin within 1 week after knee replacement, about twice a week during the first month. The APTA guideline also recommends starting therapy within 24 hours of surgery while you are still in the hospital. Your surgeon sets your exact start date, so check your discharge instructions.
How long does physical therapy last after a total knee replacement?
It varies with how quickly your motion, strength and walking return. The expert panel in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy described a first month of about two visits a week, with visit frequency in months two and three depending on whether recovery is slow, typical or fast. Your therapist and surgeon decide together when you are ready to finish.
Why is my knee still stiff weeks after knee replacement?
Stiffness is common because scar tissue forms as the knee heals and swelling limits movement. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that limited motion usually improves with physical therapy and range of motion exercises. If motion stalls, your surgeon may discuss a procedure called manipulation under anesthesia. Tell your therapist and surgeon early if bending or straightening stops improving.
Is it normal for my new knee to click?
Yes, according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, most people feel or hear some clicking of the metal and plastic parts when bending or walking. Numbness in the skin around the incision is also common. These sensations often lessen over time. New pain, swelling or a sense that the knee is giving way is different and should be reported to your surgeon.
When can I drive after knee replacement surgery?
The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says most people resume driving about 4 to 6 weeks after surgery. You need enough knee bend to sit comfortably in the car and enough muscle control to brake and accelerate quickly. Pain medicines can also affect driving. Ask your surgeon to clear you before you get behind the wheel.
Can I kneel or play sports after a knee replacement?
The AAOS notes kneeling may be uncomfortable but is not harmful. Surgeons generally advise against running, jumping and other high impact sports for the long term because they can wear the implant faster. Walking, swimming, golf, biking, light hiking and similar low impact activities are realistic goals. Your surgeon can tell you which activities fit your implant and health.
