Meniscus Tear

A torn meniscus can leave your knee sore, swollen or prone to catching, and supervised exercise is often the first step in getting it working again.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: torn meniscus, meniscal tear, torn knee cartilage, degenerative meniscus tear

What is Meniscus Tear?

Each knee has two menisci, wedge-shaped pads of tough fibrocartilage that sit between your thighbone and shinbone. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes them as shock absorbers that also help transmit weight and keep the knee stable. A tear can happen suddenly, often from twisting on a bent knee with the foot planted, or it can develop over time as the tissue weakens with age.

Athletes in contact and pivoting sports are at higher risk, but anyone can tear a meniscus. In older adults, the AAOS notes, a degenerative tear can follow something as ordinary as an awkward twist when getting up from a chair. Mayo Clinic adds that kneeling, deep squatting and lifting heavy objects can be enough. Because many athletes keep playing on a torn meniscus, symptoms sometimes build over the following days.

Common symptoms

  • A popping sensation at the time of injury
  • Pain, especially when twisting or rotating the knee
  • Swelling or stiffness that builds over the first few days
  • Catching, or a feeling that the knee is locked
  • Difficulty straightening the knee fully
  • A sense that the knee may give way
  • Reduced range of motion

Causes and risk factors

  • Twisting or turning quickly on a bent knee with the foot planted
  • Aggressive pivoting, sudden stops and cutting in sports
  • Age-related wear that makes the meniscus easier to tear
  • Kneeling, deep squatting or lifting heavy objects
  • Contact sports and sports with frequent pivoting
  • Obesity

How physical therapy helps

For degenerative tears in middle-aged adults, a randomized trial in the BMJ (Kise and colleagues, 2016) found that 12 weeks of supervised exercise therapy produced knee outcomes similar to arthroscopic partial meniscectomy at two years, with better thigh strength at three months. The APTA's 2018 clinical practice guideline on meniscal lesions recommends supervised, progressive range-of-motion exercise, knee and hip strengthening, and neuromuscular training. Your surgeon decides whether surgery is needed, and therapy has a role in either path.

Progressive strengthening

Quadriceps, hamstring and hip exercises increased step by step, since thigh weakness after a meniscus injury limits how well the knee handles load.

Range-of-motion recovery

Supervised exercises to regain full bending and straightening, which swelling and guarding often limit after a tear.

Neuromuscular training

Balance and movement-control drills so the knee stays steady during the twisting and pivoting that often caused the injury.

Swelling management

Ice, compression and short-term activity changes early on, as the APTA describes, so you can exercise comfortably.

Home program and return to activity

A home exercise plan that progresses with you and a graded return to sport or work. The APTA guideline recommends supervising that progression and teaching you to do it independently.

What to expect at your first visit

We start with how the knee was injured and what makes it catch, lock or swell. Your therapist checks for fluid in the joint, measures how far the knee bends and straightens, tests quadriceps strength and presses along the joint line for tenderness. Gentle maneuvers such as McMurray's test show which movements reproduce your symptoms. If the findings suggest you need imaging or a surgical opinion, we tell you and coordinate with your doctor.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the type and location of the tear, your age and whether surgery is part of the plan. In the BMJ trial of degenerative tears, the exercise group trained under supervision for 12 weeks. After surgery, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons estimates about 3 to 6 weeks of recovery for a partial meniscectomy and about 3 to 6 months for a meniscus repair, and the APTA notes that about four months of therapy after surgery lets many people return to their previous activities. If you have surgery, your surgeon's protocol sets the pace.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Rest from twisting, pivoting and deep squatting while symptoms are high
  • Ice the knee for about 20 minutes several times a day, as the AAOS suggests
  • Use a compression wrap and elevate the leg to help control swelling
  • Keep the knee moving gently within a comfortable range so it does not stiffen

When to see a doctor right away

  • The knee locks and you cannot straighten it
  • You cannot put weight on the leg, or the knee gives out (Mayo Clinic)
  • Marked swelling after an injury
  • Severe pain following a fall, collision or twist
  • Fever with redness, warmth and swelling around the knee
  • Persistent catching or swelling that does not settle with nonsurgical care, which the AAOS notes may need surgical evaluation

Meniscus Tear: common questions

Can a torn meniscus heal on its own?
Whether the torn tissue itself heals depends on the tear's type and location, which your doctor can explain. What research shows is that many people with degenerative tears improve without surgery. A 2016 randomized trial in the BMJ found that 12 weeks of supervised exercise worked about as well as arthroscopic partial meniscectomy at two years for middle-aged adults, and it improved thigh strength more in the short term.
Do I need surgery for a meniscus tear?
Not always. The decision belongs to you and your orthopedic surgeon and depends on the tear, your age and your symptoms. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that surgery may be considered when a tear does not respond to nonsurgical treatment. For degenerative tears without mechanical locking, research such as the 2016 BMJ trial supports trying supervised exercise first. If you do have surgery, physical therapy follows your surgeon's protocol.
Can you walk with a torn meniscus?
Many people can walk with a meniscus tear, and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that many athletes even keep playing after one. Walking may be sore, the knee may swell, and twisting or squatting often hurts more than straight-line walking. If the knee locks, will not straighten, gives way or you cannot bear weight, Mayo Clinic advises getting it checked by a doctor.
What exercises are good for a torn meniscus?
The APTA's 2018 clinical practice guideline recommends progressive range-of-motion exercises, strengthening of the knee and hip muscles, and neuromuscular training that challenges balance and control. Early on that often means quadriceps activation and gentle bending, later moving to squats, step-ups and single-leg work. Deep squats and pivoting are usually added last. A therapist can match the progression to how your knee responds.
How long does physical therapy take for a meniscus tear?
It varies. In the 2016 BMJ trial of degenerative meniscus tears, participants did 12 weeks of supervised exercise therapy. After meniscus surgery, the American Physical Therapy Association notes that about four months of physical therapy allows many people to return to their previous activity levels, including sports. Your timeline depends on the tear, any surgery, your goals and how consistently you do the home program.
Does a meniscus tear lead to arthritis?
It can raise the risk. Mayo Clinic notes that people with a torn meniscus are more likely to develop osteoarthritis in the injured knee. That does not mean arthritis is certain. Keeping the muscles around the knee strong and staying active at a sensible level are reasonable ways to support the joint over time, and a therapist can help you build a plan you can maintain.
