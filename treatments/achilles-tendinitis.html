Achilles Tendinitis (Achilles Tendinopathy)

Also called: Achilles tendinopathy, Achilles tendonitis, midportion Achilles tendinopathy, insertional Achilles tendinopathy

Achilles Tendinitis (Achilles Tendinopathy), explained

Your Achilles tendon is the largest tendon in the body, connecting the calf muscles to the heel bone. It works every time you walk, climb stairs, run or rise onto your toes. According to APTA's ChoosePT guide, Achilles tendinopathy develops when the demand on the tendon exceeds its ability to cope. You may hear it called tendinitis or tendinopathy; both names describe this painful overuse problem.

There are two main types. Midportion, or noninsertional, tendinopathy affects the middle of the tendon above the heel and is more common in younger, active people and runners. Insertional tendinopathy affects the spot where the tendon attaches to the heel bone, is often linked to calf tightness and can involve bone spurs. AAOS notes the insertional type can appear at any activity level.

Common symptoms

  • Pain and stiffness along the tendon in the morning or with the first steps
  • Pain that builds with activity, or is worse the day after exercise
  • Tenderness when the tendon or back of the heel is pressed
  • Thickening or swelling at the back of the ankle
  • Calf tightness
  • Pain on stairs, hills or when rising onto your toes

Causes and risk factors

  • A sudden jump in running distance, speed or hill work
  • Tight or weak calf muscles
  • Worn-out shoes
  • Foot shape, such as flat or high arches
  • Obesity, psoriasis, high blood pressure or high cholesterol
  • Fluoroquinolone antibiotics
  • Bone spurs at the back of the heel that rub on the tendon

How physical therapy helps

Exercise has the strongest evidence. The 2018 APTA clinical practice guideline in JOSPT recommends mechanical loading, either eccentric exercise or a heavy, slow strengthening program, to reduce pain and improve function. It also advises that complete rest is not indicated and that you can usually keep up recreational activity within your pain tolerance during rehab. A 2020 review in the Journal of Athletic Training adds that higher loads with slower contractions appear to help tendon strength most.

Progressive tendon loading

Heel raises that move from seated to standing, two legs to one, and then to slow, heavy or eccentric heel drops, matched to how your tendon responds.

Pain-guided activity plan

A pain-monitoring approach lets you stay active: mild discomfort is generally acceptable, while pain that is worse the next morning or builds day to day means scaling back.

Changes for heel-attachment pain

For insertional pain, heel raises are done from flat ground or with a lift under the heel so the tendon is not pressed against the heel bone.

Calf stretching and manual therapy

If your ankle is stiff, calf stretches with the knee straight and bent, plus joint and soft tissue mobilization, help restore motion, as the guideline suggests.

Return-to-run progression

A staged plan from walking to jogging to faster running and jumping, with strength checkpoints before full sport.

Load and footwear education

Guidance on training volume, body weight and shoe choices, the modifiable factors the guideline highlights for patient counseling.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour and includes treatment that day. Your therapist asks about your training, footwear and when the pain is worst, then presses along the tendon to find whether the sore spot is midportion or at the heel. Calf flexibility, ankle motion and strength are measured, including single-leg heel raises, and you may be asked to walk, squat, step up or hop. You leave with a starting home program.

Recovery

Achilles tendinopathy usually needs patience. AAOS notes that even with early treatment, the pain may last longer than 3 months, and a 2020 review in the Journal of Athletic Training reports that full recovery can take a year or longer, with reinjury rates of 27% to 44%, often when return to sport is rushed. Many people keep doing a scaled-down version of their activity during rehab. If symptoms persist after about 6 months of nonsurgical care, AAOS says surgery may be considered.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Swap high-impact exercise for lower-impact options such as cycling or swimming while the tendon settles.
  • Apply ice to the sore area for up to 20 minutes at a time if it eases symptoms.
  • Skip hill running and retire worn-out shoes for now.
  • Stay active within comfort instead of resting completely, since the JOSPT guideline says complete rest is not indicated.

When to see a doctor right away

  • A sudden pop or snap in the back of the calf or heel
  • Sudden severe pain, or pain that keeps you from moving or pushing off
  • Symptoms that are not improving after a few weeks
  • Tendon pain that starts while taking, or soon after, a fluoroquinolone antibiotic (contact the prescriber)

Achilles Tendinitis (Achilles Tendinopathy): common questions

Should I stop running with Achilles tendonitis?
Not always. The 2018 JOSPT guideline for Achilles tendinopathy says complete rest is not indicated for longer-standing cases and that people can usually continue recreational activity within their pain tolerance during rehab. A pain-monitoring approach helps: mild discomfort is generally acceptable, while pain or stiffness that is worse the next morning or builds day after day means cutting back distance, speed or hills.
Are eccentric heel drops the best exercise for Achilles tendinopathy?
Eccentric heel drops are one well-studied option for Achilles tendinopathy. The 2018 JOSPT guideline supports either eccentric exercise or a heavy, slow-speed strengthening program. A 2020 review in the Journal of Athletic Training notes that concentric and combined programs have also shown positive results, and that heavier, slower loading seems to build tendon strength best.
Do night splints help Achilles tendonitis?
For midportion Achilles tendinopathy, the 2018 JOSPT clinical practice guideline recommends against night splints, giving that advice its highest grade of evidence. Some sources, including AAOS, still list night splints among nonsurgical options. Because the guideline evidence points elsewhere, most physical therapy plans for Achilles tendinopathy focus on progressive loading exercise instead of splinting.
Can Achilles tendonitis turn into a rupture?
Mayo Clinic notes that Achilles tendinitis can weaken the tendon and make a tear more likely, which is a serious injury that often needs surgery. Warning signs of a rupture include a sudden pop or snap in the back of the calf or heel, or sudden severe pain that makes walking or pushing off difficult. If that happens, see a doctor right away.
Do heel lifts help Achilles pain?
The evidence is mixed. The 2018 JOSPT guideline makes no recommendation on heel lifts for midportion Achilles tendinopathy because studies disagree. For insertional Achilles pain near the heel bone, a 2020 review in the Journal of Athletic Training suggests limiting how far the ankle bends during heel raises, for example by placing a small lift under the heel, to avoid compressing the tendon.
Who is most likely to get Achilles tendinopathy?
Runners and other active people are at higher risk. The 2018 JOSPT guideline cites a large records review in which adults aged 40 to 59 were diagnosed most often, and reports a prevalence of 6.2% to 9.5% in general runners. Mayo Clinic adds older age, tight calves, flat or high arches, obesity and certain antibiotics as risk factors.
