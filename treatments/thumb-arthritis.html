Thumb Arthritis (Basal Joint / CMC Arthritis)

Pain at the base of your thumb when you pinch, grip, or open a jar? Hand therapy protects the joint and helps you keep doing the tasks you rely on.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: basal joint arthritis, CMC arthritis, carpometacarpal joint arthritis, thumb base osteoarthritis

What is Thumb Arthritis (Basal Joint / CMC Arthritis)?

The joint where your thumb meets the wrist is called the carpometacarpal, or CMC, joint. It lets the thumb swivel, pivot, and pinch, and it takes a great deal of force over a lifetime of use. Over time the smooth cartilage covering the bone ends wears away, the bones rub, and the joint becomes painful. A bony bump may form at the base of the thumb.

According to the AAOS, women are 2 to 3 times more likely than men to develop thumb arthritis, and it is most common after age 50. The AAOS lists family history, extra body weight, and an old thumb or wrist injury as risk factors, along with jobs that involve heavy gripping or lifting, and Mayo Clinic adds inherited ligament looseness and notes that rheumatoid arthritis can also damage the cartilage in this joint.

Common symptoms

  • Pain at the base of the thumb when you pinch, grip, or turn things
  • Weak pinch or grip, such as trouble opening jars or turning keys
  • Swelling and tenderness at the base of the thumb
  • A bony bump or enlarged look at the joint
  • Stiffness and reduced thumb motion
  • A deep ache after heavy hand use

Causes and risk factors

  • Age-related cartilage wear (osteoarthritis)
  • Female sex
  • A previous thumb fracture or sprain
  • Family history or loose ligaments
  • Jobs or hobbies with heavy pinching and gripping
  • Excess body weight
  • Rheumatoid arthritis

How hand therapy helps

Hand therapy for thumb arthritis aims to reduce pain while keeping your hand useful. The 2019 American College of Rheumatology and Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends hand orthoses for people with first CMC joint arthritis and conditionally recommends local heat or cold. The American Society of Hand Therapists describes custom orthoses, adaptive tools, and home exercise programs designed to protect the joints, decrease stiffness, and improve hand use. The AAOS adds that gentle exercises can improve motion and strengthen thumb muscles.

Custom thumb orthosis

Laura Drumm, CHT, fabricates a splint in the clinic that supports the thumb base during painful tasks, overnight, or both. The ACR guideline rates orthoses for this joint as strongly recommended.

Joint protection training

We show you ways to spread force across the whole hand and avoid tight, sustained pinches that load the CMC joint.

Thumb motion and strengthening

Gentle exercises, which the AAOS supports, help maintain range of motion and build the muscles that support the thumb base.

Adaptive tools

Mayo Clinic recommends aids such as jar openers and key turners. We help you choose the ones that match your daily tasks.

Heat or cold at home

The ACR guideline conditionally recommends local heat or cold for hand arthritis, and we teach you how to use either safely.

Rehab after thumb surgery

After a thumb base procedure, we follow your surgeon's protocol and coordinate with the surgeon as motion and strength return.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit lasts about an hour and includes evaluation and treatment. Laura asks which daily tasks hurt, such as opening jars, writing, or turning keys. She examines the thumb base for swelling, tenderness, and a bony bump, measures thumb motion, and tests grip and pinch strength. She also looks at how you hold objects and checks the nearby joints, then fits a splint and starts a joint protection and exercise plan.

Recovery

Therapy cannot regrow worn cartilage, so the goal is less pain and better use of your hand. How much relief you get depends on the stage of the joint changes and how consistently you use your splint and joint protection habits. Symptoms can come and go with activity, and the plan is adjusted over time. If nonsurgical care stops controlling pain, a hand surgeon may discuss surgery. The AAOS notes that full recovery after surgery takes several months, and grip strength can keep improving for 9 to 12 months.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Ice the thumb base for 5 to 15 minutes several times a day, as the AAOS suggests, or try heat if it feels better, as Mayo Clinic notes some people prefer.
  • Use jar openers, key turners, and larger-handled tools to cut down on tight pinching.
  • Avoid the activities that clearly flare the pain while you wait to be seen.
  • Topical or oral anti-inflammatory medicine may help if your doctor approves it.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Swelling, stiffness, or pain at the base of the thumb that continues despite home care (Mayo Clinic)
  • Swelling and stiffness in many joints at once, since Mayo Clinic notes rheumatoid arthritis can also damage thumb cartilage and needs medical management
  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb and fingers, which points to a nerve problem such as carpal tunnel syndrome rather than arthritis

Thumb Arthritis (Basal Joint / CMC Arthritis): common questions

What is the best brace for thumb arthritis?
The 2019 American College of Rheumatology and Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends hand orthoses for arthritis at the base of the thumb. The best choice depends on which tasks hurt, whether you wear it at night or during activity, and your hand size. A certified hand therapist can make a custom orthosis or help you pick a fitted one.
Can thumb arthritis be reversed?
No. The AAOS explains that thumb arthritis happens when the cartilage at the base of the thumb wears away, and that cartilage does not grow back. Treatment focuses on easing pain and keeping the hand working. Splinting, joint protection, exercise, heat or cold, and medicine from your doctor can all help, and surgery is an option when nonsurgical care no longer controls symptoms.
Should I exercise an arthritic thumb or rest it?
Usually some of both. The AAOS recommends avoiding the activities that aggravate thumb arthritis while also doing gentle exercises to improve range of motion and strengthen thumb muscles. Resting the joint in a splint during painful tasks can make exercise more comfortable. A hand therapist can show you which movements to do and which pinching and gripping patterns to limit.
Is heat or ice better for thumb arthritis pain?
Either can help, and personal preference matters. The AAOS and Mayo Clinic suggest ice on the thumb base for 5 to 15 minutes several times a day for pain and swelling. Mayo Clinic notes that some people find heat works better, such as heat wraps or warm paraffin. The ACR guideline conditionally recommends local heat or cold for hand arthritis.
When is surgery needed for thumb arthritis?
Surgery is generally considered when splints, activity changes, medicine, and injections no longer control pain, a decision made with a hand surgeon. Mayo Clinic describes injections as an option when other conservative measures fail. The AAOS reports that full recovery from thumb base surgery takes several months, with grip strength improving for up to 9 to 12 months, and hand therapy usually follows.
Who is most likely to get thumb arthritis?
According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, women are 2 to 3 times more likely than men to develop arthritis at the base of the thumb, and it is most common after age 50. The AAOS also lists family history, excess weight, previous thumb or wrist injuries, and work that requires repetitive gripping or heavy lifting, and Mayo Clinic adds inherited loose ligaments and rheumatoid arthritis.
