Head & Neck

Whiplash

Whiplash can leave your neck stiff, sore and slow to turn, and guided physical therapy helps you move again safely while the injured tissues recover.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: whiplash-associated disorder, WAD, cervical sprain, neck sprain

Whiplash, explained

Whiplash is a neck injury caused by a fast back-and-forth snap of the head, like the crack of a whip. In that split second, the muscles, ligaments and small joints of the neck are pushed past what they can comfortably handle. StatPearls (NCBI) describes the neck briefly bending into an S shape, which strains the front of the spine and jams the small facet joints at the back.

Rear-end car crashes are the classic cause. StatPearls reports that motor vehicle collisions account for about 86.4% of cases and that almost two thirds of cases involve women. Whiplash can also come from contact sports, falls, amusement rides or an assault. According to Mayo Clinic, symptoms usually start within days of the injury, so it is common to feel fine at the scene.

Common symptoms

  • Neck pain and stiffness
  • Pain that gets worse when you move your neck
  • Less ability to turn or tilt your head
  • Headaches that often start at the base of the skull
  • Tenderness in the shoulders, upper back or arms
  • Tingling or numbness in the arms
  • Tiredness or dizziness
  • Trouble sleeping, concentrating or remembering things

Causes and risk factors

  • Rear-end car collisions, the most common cause
  • Tackles and collisions in contact sports
  • Falls in which the head snaps backward and forward
  • Physical assault
  • Amusement park rides such as roller coasters
  • A previous whiplash injury, older age, existing neck or back pain, and high-speed crashes, which Mayo Clinic links to slower recovery

How physical therapy helps

Guidelines favor early, gentle movement over rest. The 2017 neck pain clinical practice guideline in JOSPT advises returning to normal, non-aggravating activities as soon as possible, keeping collar use to a minimum and doing posture and mobility exercises. It also recommends a comprehensive strength and endurance exercise program, and for longer-lasting symptoms it lists hands-on mobilization combined with progressive exercise as an option. StatPearls (NCBI) adds that supervised exercise programs reduce pain and disability after whiplash.

Early guided movement

Gentle range-of-motion and posture exercises start as soon as it is safe, since Mayo Clinic notes that keeping the neck still for long periods can weaken muscles and slow recovery.

Manual mobilization

Hands-on mobilization of stiff neck and upper back segments is paired with exercise, which the JOSPT guideline lists as an option when symptoms last longer.

Strength and endurance training

Exercises for the neck and shoulder girdle muscles rebuild the support your neck needs for driving, desk work and lifting.

Coordination and functional practice

You work on head control and real tasks, such as turning to check a blind spot, so everyday movements feel steady again.

Education and pacing

You learn what is expected after a neck sprain, how to pace activity, and how to handle pain flares, which the guideline supports alongside exercise.

Progress monitoring

Your therapist rechecks pain, motion and function over time so a slower-than-expected recovery is spotted early and your plan can be adjusted.

What to expect at your first visit

Your therapist starts with how the injury happened and how your symptoms have changed since. The exam measures neck range of motion, checks strength, sensation and reflexes in your arms, looks at posture and muscle tenderness, and screens for warning signs that call for imaging or a physician first. Short questionnaires about pain and daily function set a baseline so progress can be measured at later visits.

Recovery

Most people improve. Mayo Clinic says most people with whiplash get better within a few weeks by following a treatment plan, and Cleveland Clinic notes that more severe injuries can take several weeks or even months to heal. Some people have lasting symptoms. StatPearls (NCBI) reports that about one quarter of patients develop chronic symptoms and that symptoms persisting beyond 3 months point toward a slower recovery. Your therapist watches for that pattern and adjusts your care if progress stalls.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Rest for a day or two if you need to, then ease back into gentle everyday movement, since Mayo Clinic notes that too much bed rest may slow healing.
  • Use heat or cold on your neck for about 15 minutes every three hours or so.
  • Avoid wearing a soft collar for long periods unless a physician has told you to.
  • Take frequent breaks from screens and long drives, and change position often.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Any neck pain after a car crash, sports injury or fall should be checked by a physician to rule out a fracture or other damage.
  • Severe neck pain with weakness in your arms or legs, or trouble walking, needs emergency care.
  • Tenderness directly over the bones at the back of the neck, or tingling in the arms after an injury, should be evaluated by a physician before therapy.
  • Adults over 65 with neck pain after a crash or fall should see a physician first, as imaging rules flag this group.
  • A high-energy or otherwise dangerous mechanism of injury warrants medical evaluation before exercise.

Whiplash: common questions

How long does whiplash last?
The 2017 neck pain guideline published in JOSPT advises clinicians to reassure patients that recovery from whiplash is expected within the first 2 to 3 months. Cleveland Clinic notes that minor injuries can recover within days or a few weeks. A smaller group has symptoms for longer, which is why progress should be tracked so care can be adjusted if recovery is slower than expected.
Why does whiplash pain show up a day or two after an accident?
Delayed symptoms are common with whiplash. Mayo Clinic says symptoms most often start within days of the injury, and Cleveland Clinic notes they can appear right away or take up to several days to develop. Because of that delay, it makes sense to get checked after a crash even if you felt fine at the scene, and to report any new symptoms.
Should I wear a neck brace after whiplash?
Usually only briefly, if at all. Mayo Clinic explains that soft foam collars were once common, but studies show that keeping the neck still for long periods can decrease muscle strength and slow recovery. Some experts limit collar use to about 72 hours. The JOSPT neck pain guideline also advises minimizing collar use. Follow your physician's instructions if a collar was prescribed for a specific reason.
Is physical therapy good for whiplash?
Physical therapy is a standard part of whiplash care. StatPearls (NCBI) reports that supervised exercise programs reduce pain and disability, and the JOSPT neck pain guideline recommends education, early movement and exercise, and lists manual mobilization combined with exercise as an option for longer-lasting symptoms. A physical therapist also checks for warning signs, such as arm weakness, that need a physician's attention.
Can whiplash cause headaches and dizziness?
Yes. Mayo Clinic lists headaches, most often starting at the base of the skull, along with dizziness and fatigue among common whiplash symptoms. Some people also notice blurred vision, ringing in the ears, poor sleep, irritability or trouble concentrating. These symptoms are worth mentioning to your physician and therapist, since they help shape the exam and the treatment plan.
What are the grades of whiplash?
Clinicians often use the Quebec Task Force scale, described in StatPearls (NCBI). Grade 1 is neck pain or stiffness without exam findings. Grade 2 adds musculoskeletal signs such as reduced motion or tenderness. Grade 3 includes neurologic signs like weakness, sensory changes or reflex changes. Grade 4 involves a fracture or dislocation. Grade 0 means no neck complaints at all.
