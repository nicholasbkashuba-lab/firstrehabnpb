Trigger Finger

A finger that catches, clicks, or locks can turn gripping into a chore. Hand therapy and a custom splint give the irritated tendon a chance to settle down.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: stenosing tenosynovitis, trigger thumb, locking finger

Trigger Finger, explained

Each finger is bent by flexor tendons that run from the forearm muscles to the finger bones. Along the way they pass through a series of tunnels called pulleys. The AAOS identifies the A1 pulley, which sits in the palm at the base of each finger, as the one most involved in trigger finger. When the tendon or its sheath swells, it stops gliding smoothly through that pulley. It catches, then snaps free like a trigger.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons reports that trigger finger affects about 2% of the general population, most often women between 40 and 60. Any finger or the thumb can be involved, and Cleveland Clinic notes that one in three people have it in more than one finger at a time. People with diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, or amyloidosis are at higher risk, as are people who grip or pinch repeatedly for work or hobbies.

Common symptoms

  • Catching, popping, or clicking when you bend or straighten the finger
  • A finger that locks bent, then suddenly pops straight
  • A tender bump in the palm at the base of the finger
  • Stiffness that is worst first thing in the morning
  • Pain at the base of the finger when gripping
  • A finger stuck in a bent position in more advanced cases

Causes and risk factors

  • Repetitive gripping or pinching at work or in hobbies
  • Diabetes
  • Rheumatoid arthritis, gout, or osteoarthritis
  • Amyloidosis or thyroid disease
  • Being female and between about 40 and 60

How hand therapy helps

Conservative care for trigger finger centers on resting the tendon so the swelling at the pulley can calm down. The AAOS lists splinting and rest from repetitive gripping as first steps. The American Society of Hand Therapists notes that certified hand therapists make custom orthoses, teach exercises to prevent stiffness, and suggest activity changes during healing. A StatPearls review on the NIH Bookshelf describes a common approach: a splint that holds the knuckle at the base of the finger slightly bent for 6 to 10 weeks.

Custom finger orthosis

Laura Drumm, CHT, fabricates a small splint in the clinic that limits motion at the base of the affected finger, so the tendon stops sliding back and forth through the tight pulley.

Night positioning

The AAOS notes that wearing a splint at night to keep the finger straight can help, and it may ease the morning stiffness many people notice.

Gentle motion exercises

Exercises keep the finger joints from stiffening while the tendon rests, part of the nonsurgical care ASHT describes.

Grip and activity changes

We look at how you hold tools, steering wheels, and phones, then suggest changes that cut down on repeated pressure over the palm.

Rehab after release surgery

If you have a trigger finger release, we follow your surgeon's protocol to restore motion and keep the surgeon informed of your progress.

What to expect at your first visit

Your first visit runs about an hour, with evaluation and treatment the same day. Laura feels for the tender nodule in your palm and watches the finger as you make a fist and open it, noting whether it catches, locks, or needs help to straighten. She measures finger motion and grip strength and asks about morning stiffness and other fingers. Then she fits your custom splint and teaches your home program.

Recovery

Timelines vary with how long the finger has been triggering and how often it locks. The StatPearls review describes splinting programs of 6 to 10 weeks, and it cites a randomized study in which people treated with a splint, a steroid injection, or both had similar pain relief and function at one year. If the finger keeps locking, your physician may talk with you about an injection or surgery. After a release, the AAOS says the incision usually heals within a few weeks, but swelling and stiffness can take 4 to 6 months to fully go away.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Give the finger a break from forceful or repeated gripping when you can.
  • Move the finger gently through its range a few times a day to limit stiffness.
  • Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medicine may ease pain if your doctor says it is safe for you.
  • Keep a note of which activities bring on catching so you can share it at your evaluation.

When to see a doctor right away

  • A finger you suddenly cannot move or use, which Cleveland Clinic says warrants a trip to the emergency room
  • Pain, stiffness, or loss of motion that keeps getting worse
  • Catching or locking in a child's thumb or finger, which the AAOS describes as a separate congenital condition that needs a physician's evaluation

Trigger Finger: common questions

Can trigger finger go away on its own?
Mild trigger finger sometimes settles with rest from gripping, which the AAOS lists as a first-line treatment. However, the AAOS also notes that symptoms often start slowly and get worse over time. The American Society of Hand Therapists describes custom orthoses, exercises to avoid stiffness and activity changes as nonsurgical hand therapy care. Early care, such as a splint and activity changes, gives the tendon a chance to calm down.
How long should I wear a trigger finger splint?
Wear time depends on your symptoms and the type of splint. A StatPearls review describes a common approach of a splint holding the base knuckle slightly bent for 6 to 10 weeks, and the AAOS mentions night splinting to keep the finger straight. Your hand therapist sets a schedule for day and night wear and adjusts it as the catching improves.
Is a splint as effective as a cortisone shot for trigger finger?
Both are common first treatments. A StatPearls review cites a randomized study that found no significant difference in pain relief or function at one year between steroid injection alone, splinting alone, or the two combined. Injections are given by a physician, while splints are made and fitted by a hand therapist. Your doctor can help you weigh the options for your situation.
Do exercises help trigger finger?
Gentle exercises can help keep the finger from stiffening while the tendon rests. Cleveland Clinic lists stretching exercises among nonsurgical options, and the American Society of Hand Therapists notes that hand therapists teach exercises to prevent stiffness. The aim is easy, controlled motion, and a locked finger should never be forced straight. A therapist can show you which movements help and which ones irritate the pulley.
Why is trigger finger worse in the morning?
The AAOS explains that trigger finger stiffness tends to be worse after periods of inactivity, such as overnight sleep. Fingers often rest in a curled position at night, and the swollen tendon has to work through the tight pulley once you start moving. Night splinting that keeps the finger straight is one strategy the AAOS mentions for this pattern.
Do I need hand therapy after trigger finger release surgery?
It depends on how your finger moves in the weeks after surgery, and your surgeon decides whether therapy is needed. The American Society of Hand Therapists notes that certified hand therapists help restore hand function and range of motion after surgery. The AAOS reports that swelling and stiffness can take 4 to 6 months to fully resolve, so some stiffness in the early months is common.
