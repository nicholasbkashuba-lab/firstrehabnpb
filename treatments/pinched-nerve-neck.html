Pinched Nerve in the Neck (Cervical Radiculopathy)

A pinched nerve in your neck can send pain, tingling or weakness down your arm, and physical therapy aims to ease pressure on that nerve and restore comfortable movement.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: cervical radiculopathy, pinched nerve in neck, cervical nerve root compression

Pinched Nerve in the Neck (Cervical Radiculopathy), explained

Your neck (the cervical spine) is a stack of seven small vertebrae with cushioning disks between them. At each level, a nerve root branches off the spinal cord and leaves through a small opening on its way to your shoulder, arm and hand. Cervical radiculopathy happens when one of these nerve roots is squeezed or irritated, so symptoms follow the path of that nerve instead of staying in the neck.

It is most common in adults. According to Cleveland Clinic, herniated disks are the more frequent cause in people under 50, disk wear is typical in people in their 50s and 60s, and arthritis that narrows the nerve openings is more common after 70. StatPearls (NCBI) reports that the C7 nerve root is involved in more than half of cases, with C6 next most often affected.

Common symptoms

  • Pain that spreads from the neck into the shoulder blade, arm or hand
  • Sharp or burning pain, usually on one side
  • Tingling or pins and needles in the fingers or hand
  • Numbness in part of the arm or hand
  • Weakness in the shoulder, arm or hand muscles
  • Pain that gets worse with certain neck movements
  • Some relief when you rest your hand on top of your head

Causes and risk factors

  • A herniated disk pressing on a nerve root, sometimes after lifting, pulling, bending or twisting
  • Age-related disk wear that lowers disk height and causes bulging
  • Bone spurs from neck arthritis narrowing the opening where the nerve exits
  • Heavy manual labor, a lot of driving, or regular use of vibrating equipment
  • A history of smoking

How physical therapy helps

Most pinched nerves in the neck improve without surgery, and therapy aims to calm the nerve while restoring motion and strength. APTA's ChoosePT guide states that physical therapy is an effective treatment for cervical radiculopathy and that in many cases it resolves symptoms. The 2017 neck pain clinical practice guideline in JOSPT recommends combining stretching and strengthening exercise with neck and upper back mobilization and traction for longer-lasting cases, plus education that keeps you active at work and in exercise.

Manual traction and mobilization

Gentle hands-on traction can take pressure off the irritated nerve root, and mobilizing stiff joints in the neck and upper back helps restore movement that pain has limited.

Progressive exercise

You begin with easy range-of-motion work and move toward strengthening the neck, shoulder blade and arm muscles as arm symptoms settle.

Posture and workstation changes

Your therapist reviews how you sit, sleep and work, then suggests adjustments that reduce strain on the nerve during the hours you spend outside the clinic.

Education and activity guidance

You learn which positions ease arm symptoms, which ones aggravate them, and how to keep working and exercising while the nerve calms down.

Home exercise program

A short daily routine keeps progress going between visits and gives you a plan for managing a flare if symptoms return later.

What to expect at your first visit

Your evaluation starts with where your symptoms travel and what makes them better or worse. Your therapist gently tests neck motion and checks reflexes, sensation and strength in both arms. Special tests may include the Spurling test, which StatPearls (NCBI) describes as useful for confirming nerve root involvement. Treatment starts in that same first hour, and you leave with a home program.

Recovery

Recovery from a pinched nerve varies from person to person. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, pain goes away in days or weeks for some people and takes longer for others. Cleveland Clinic reports that over 85% of cases resolve within 8 to 12 weeks and that about 90% of people have good to excellent outcomes with nonsurgical treatment. Your therapist rechecks arm symptoms, strength and reflexes as you go and adjusts your plan, letting your physician know if anything changes.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Use ice or heat on your neck and shoulder blade area for comfort, whichever feels better.
  • Stay gently active and avoid holding your head in one position for long stretches.
  • Set up your desk and screen so you are not leaning your head forward for hours at a time.
  • If resting your hand on top of your head eases the arm pain, use that position for short breaks.
  • Hold off on heavy lifting and movements that clearly send more pain down your arm until you are evaluated.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Neck pain after a car accident, fall or diving injury needs emergency evaluation.
  • Weakness in your arms or legs, or trouble walking, needs emergency care.
  • Severe neck pain with a high fever needs emergency care.
  • Fever, night sweats or unexplained weight loss along with neck or arm pain should be checked promptly by a physician.
  • Constant pain at rest that does not change with position should be checked by a physician.
  • Arm symptoms that last more than a week, or any muscle weakness or change in reflexes, warrant a visit to your physician.

Pinched Nerve in the Neck (Cervical Radiculopathy): common questions

Can a pinched nerve in the neck heal on its own?
Often, yes. StatPearls (NCBI) reports that over 85% of acute cervical radiculopathy cases resolve without any specific treatment within 8 to 12 weeks. Many people still choose physical therapy to ease pain sooner, rebuild strength and learn how to protect the neck. Weakness, spreading numbness or symptoms that keep getting worse should be checked by a physician instead of waiting them out.
Is physical therapy good for a pinched nerve in the neck?
Physical therapy is a common first-line treatment for a pinched nerve in the neck. APTA's ChoosePT guide says physical therapy is effective for cervical radiculopathy and that most cases resolve without surgery. Care usually includes hands-on traction and mobilization, exercises that progress from gentle motion to strengthening, and posture changes. A physical therapist also screens for signs that need a physician's attention.
Why does a pinched nerve in my neck cause arm pain?
Each nerve root that leaves the neck supplies a particular strip of the shoulder, arm and hand. When a disk or bone spur presses on that root, symptoms can show up anywhere along the nerve's route. That is why burning pain, tingling or weakness can appear in the forearm or fingers while the neck itself feels only mildly sore, or not sore at all.
What should I avoid with a pinched nerve in my neck?
It helps to limit activities that clearly send more pain down the arm, such as heavy lifting, long sessions at a laptop with your head pushed forward, or sleeping with your neck twisted. Cleveland Clinic advises that a soft cervical collar, if used at all, be worn for 1 to 2 weeks at most. Gentle movement usually helps more than complete rest.
Can a pinched nerve in the neck come back?
It can. Cleveland Clinic reports that symptoms return in up to one third of people after they first improve. Keeping the neck and upper back strong, taking movement breaks during desk work, and returning to your home exercises at the first sign of tingling are reasonable ways to manage that risk. A new episode with weakness should be checked by a physician.
When is surgery needed for a pinched nerve in the neck?
Most people with a pinched nerve in the neck never need surgery. Cleveland Clinic says surgery may be considered when nonsurgical treatment has not worked after 6 to 12 weeks. A spine surgeon makes that decision based on symptoms, exam findings and imaging. During therapy, your physical therapist tracks strength and reflexes and lets your physician know if they are changing.
