Lower Back & Spine

Herniated Disc

Physical therapy for a herniated or bulging disc in the lower back, focused on easing nerve pain and getting you back to bending, lifting and moving with confidence.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: slipped disc, ruptured disc, bulging disc, lumbar disc herniation, herniated nucleus pulposus

What is Herniated Disc?

Between each pair of bones in your spine sits a disc with a tough outer ring and a soft, gel-like center. These discs act like shock absorbers when you walk, run or jump. A herniation happens when part of the soft center pushes through a tear in the outer ring. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, the displaced material can press on nearby nerve roots and release chemicals that inflame them.

In the lower back, the discs between L4 and L5 and between L5 and S1 account for about 95% of herniations in adults aged 25 to 55, according to StatPearls. The same reference reports 5 to 20 new cases per 1,000 adults each year, mostly between ages 30 and 50, with men affected about twice as often as women. Age-related drying of the discs makes them less flexible and more likely to tear.

Common symptoms

  • Low back pain, often the first thing you notice
  • Sharp, shooting pain from the buttock down the back of one leg
  • Numbness or tingling in the leg or foot
  • Weakness in leg or foot muscles
  • Pain that worsens with sitting, bending or certain movements
  • Stiffness that makes it hard to bend or straighten up

Causes and risk factors

  • Age-related loss of water in the discs, which makes them less flexible
  • Lifting with the back instead of the legs, especially while twisting
  • Repetitive straining at work or in sports
  • Excess body weight
  • Long hours of driving or sitting
  • Smoking
  • A fall or other injury to the back

How physical therapy helps

Most lumbar disc herniations improve without surgery. StatPearls reports that over 85% of acute herniated disc symptoms resolve within 8 to 12 weeks with conservative management. The 2021 JOSPT low back pain guideline supports exercise training, including trunk strengthening, endurance and specific trunk muscle activation, for back pain with leg pain. The APTA ChoosePT herniated disk guide describes a plan built around pain reduction, posture, flexibility, core strength and safe body mechanics for daily tasks.

Symptom-easing positions

Early in care, your therapist looks for positions and movements that reduce leg symptoms so you can stay active while the irritated nerve calms down.

Core strengthening

Progressive work for the deep abdominal, back and hip muscles helps the spine handle sitting, standing and lifting with less irritation.

Flexibility and motion exercises

Gentle stretching and range of motion work restores movement in the lower back and hips that pain has limited.

Body mechanics training

You practice bending, lifting, carrying and getting in and out of the car in ways that ease strain on the healing disc.

Endurance and return to activity

As pain settles, your program builds staying power for work, exercise and longer days on your feet.

What to expect at your first visit

Plan on about an hour. Your therapist starts with questions about how the pain began, where it travels and which positions make it better or worse. Testing covers lower back movement, muscle strength and tightness, numbness, reflexes, joint stiffness, posture and walking. Your therapist watches how leg symptoms respond as you move, which guides the first exercises. You begin treatment the same day and go home with a written program.

Recovery

Timelines vary with the size of the herniation, how long symptoms have been present and your general health. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons states that most patients are free of symptoms by 3 to 4 months. Cleveland Clinic notes that many people feel better within about a month of conservative care. StatPearls adds that about 30% of patients still have some back pain a year later, so ongoing exercise and good lifting habits remain worthwhile after formal therapy ends.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Rest for a day or two if needed, then return to gentle movement
  • Notice which positions ease your leg pain and use them through the day
  • Avoid heavy lifting and bending while twisting
  • Change positions often instead of sitting for long stretches
  • Try ice or heat on your lower back for comfort

When to see a doctor right away

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control (a medical emergency called cauda equina syndrome)
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs
  • Tingling, numbness or weakness that is getting steadily worse
  • Difficulty standing or walking because of leg weakness
  • Back pain after a significant fall or accident

Herniated Disc: common questions

Can a herniated disc heal on its own?
Many do improve without surgery. StatPearls, a National Library of Medicine reference, reports that over 85% of acute herniated disc symptoms resolve within 8 to 12 weeks with conservative care such as activity changes, medication from your physician and physical therapy. Improvement is more likely when you stay gently active. A physical therapist can guide which movements to use while symptoms settle.
Is a bulging disc the same as a herniated disc?
The terms are often used interchangeably, and Cleveland Clinic lists bulging disk among the other names for a herniated disk. On imaging reports, however, radiologists frequently describe a bulge and a herniation or protrusion as separate findings. Either can be present without pain, so the report alone does not decide treatment. Your symptoms and exam matter more.
Why does my MRI show a disc bulge if I have no pain there?
Disc changes are very common in people without back pain. A 2015 systematic review in the American Journal of Neuroradiology found disc bulges on imaging in 30% of pain-free 20-year-olds and 84% of pain-free 80-year-olds. The authors concluded that many of these findings may be part of normal aging. That is why therapists match imaging to your actual symptoms.
Will I need surgery for a herniated disc?
Most people do not. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons recommends trying nonsurgical treatment such as physical therapy first, and surgery is generally considered when symptoms persist despite that care or when nerve weakness is progressing. Loss of bladder or bowel control is different: it is an emergency and needs immediate medical attention.
Can a herniated disc happen again?
It can. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that after surgery there is up to a 20 to 25% chance the disc will herniate again during your lifetime. Without surgery, the APTA ChoosePT guide points to habits that lower strain on the spine, including good posture, safe lifting, a healthy weight, not smoking and staying physically active.
Can physical therapy make a herniated disc worse?
A good program is adjusted to how your symptoms respond. Your therapist checks how leg pain, numbness and strength change as you move and exercise, and changes the plan if symptoms spread farther down the leg or get worse. Some soreness from new exercise can happen, but increasing leg numbness or weakness should be reported to your therapist and physician right away.
