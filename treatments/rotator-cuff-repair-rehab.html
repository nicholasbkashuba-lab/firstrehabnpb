Rehab After Rotator Cuff Repair

Shoulder rehab after rotator cuff repair that moves through your surgeon's protocol one phase at a time, protecting the repair while you regain motion and strength.

Book an Evaluation →

Also called: rotator cuff surgery rehab, physical therapy after rotator cuff repair, post-op rotator cuff rehabilitation, arthroscopic rotator cuff repair recovery

About the surgery, and why rehab matters

The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles whose tendons wrap around the ball of the shoulder and keep it centered as you lift and reach. In a rotator cuff repair, the surgeon stitches a torn tendon back onto the top of the upper arm bone (the humerus). MedlinePlus explains that this can be done through small arthroscopic incisions, a larger open incision, or a combination called a mini open repair.

Rehab matters because the repaired tendon needs weeks of protection while it heals to bone, yet a shoulder kept still too long tends to stiffen. Physical therapy balances those two needs. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes a staged program: a sling period, passive motion with the therapist moving your arm, active motion you control yourself, then strengthening. Your surgeon decides when you move from one phase to the next.

What rehab works on

  • Shoulder stiffness, especially reaching overhead or behind your back
  • Pain at night and trouble finding a comfortable sleeping position
  • Weakness lifting or rotating the arm
  • Swelling and soreness around the shoulder after surgery
  • Difficulty with dressing, bathing and reaching shelves
  • Shoulder blade and posture changes from weeks in a sling
  • Neck and upper back tightness from guarding the arm

Why people have this surgery

  • A full thickness rotator cuff tear that causes pain and weakness
  • Shoulder pain that does not improve with nonsurgical care, often after 6 to 12 months of symptoms
  • A tear larger than about 3 centimeters with good surrounding tendon quality
  • Significant weakness and loss of shoulder function
  • A recent tear from a fall or other acute injury
  • Overhead work or sports that demand a strong shoulder

How physical therapy helps

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, rehabilitation after rotator cuff repair moves through phases: about 4 to 6 weeks in a sling, passive motion during that early period, active motion after roughly 4 to 6 weeks, and resistance exercise starting around 8 to 12 weeks. A 2017 systematic review of meta-analyses in The American Journal of Sports Medicine found that starting motion early improves range of motion but increases the risk of the tendon tearing again. That tradeoff is why your therapist follows your surgeon's timing closely.

Passive range of motion

In the early phase your therapist moves your arm for you, within the limits your surgeon sets, to limit stiffness without loading the repair.

Active assisted and active motion

When your surgeon clears it, you begin lifting and rotating the arm yourself, often with help from your other arm or a stick at first.

Shoulder blade and posture work

Exercises for the muscles around the shoulder blade and upper back that support the arm during reaching.

Progressive strengthening

Resistance exercises for the rotator cuff and shoulder muscles, added in the later phase the AAOS describes and progressed gradually.

Sling and daily task coaching

How to get in and out of the sling, sleep, dress and bathe while keeping the repair protected.

Home exercise program

A short routine done several times a day that changes as you move through each phase.

What to expect at your first visit

Plan on about an hour for your first visit, with treatment started that same day. Your therapist reviews your surgeon's protocol, including the type and size of the repair and any motion limits. They check the incision area and swelling, then measure shoulder motion within safe limits, often with your arm moved for you. They also look at your neck, elbow, wrist and hand, review sling use and sleep positions, and teach your first home exercises.

Recovery

Rotator cuff recovery is slow because tendon heals to bone gradually. MedlinePlus says recovery can take 4 to 6 months, depending on the size of the tear, and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that most people reach functional motion and adequate strength by 4 to 6 months, with complete recovery taking longer. MedlinePlus adds that results tend to be less favorable for people over 65, smokers, those with large tears, and those who do not follow post surgery instructions. Your surgeon guides each phase.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Wear your sling exactly as your surgeon instructs, including at night if told to.
  • Do only the exercises you were given and stay within the motion limits your surgeon set.
  • Do not lift, push or pull with the operated arm until your surgeon clears it.
  • Try sleeping semi reclined with a pillow supporting your arm if lying flat is uncomfortable, and ask your surgeon what positions are allowed.
  • Ask your surgeon or therapist before you drive, since MedlinePlus notes you may not be able to drive for a few weeks.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Numbness or tingling in your fingers or hand that does not go away
  • Hand or fingers that look discolored or feel cool
  • Fever above 101°F, or redness, swelling or yellowish drainage at the incision
  • A dressing that keeps soaking through with blood even when you press on it
  • Swelling of the arm or pain that your medicine does not control
  • A sudden pop, new weakness or sharp pain after a fall or lifting, which can signal a problem with the repair

Rehab After Rotator Cuff Repair: common questions

How long do you wear a sling after rotator cuff surgery?
MedlinePlus and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons both describe wearing a sling for about 4 to 6 weeks after rotator cuff repair. The sling protects the tendon while it starts healing to bone. Your surgeon may adjust the time based on the size of the tear and the quality of the tissue, so follow your own instructions.
When does physical therapy start after rotator cuff repair?
Many protocols start passive motion within the first few weeks, with a therapist moving the arm for you while the sling protects the repair at other times. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons places passive exercise within the first 4 to 6 weeks. The exact start date depends on your surgeon, so bring your protocol to your first therapy visit.
Why can't I lift my arm on my own after rotator cuff surgery?
In the early weeks you are not supposed to lift the arm with your own muscles, because that pulls on the freshly repaired tendon. According to the AAOS, active motion usually begins after about 4 to 6 weeks, once some healing has occurred. Until then, your therapist moves your arm passively. Lifting on your own too early can stress the repair.
Is it normal to have a stiff shoulder months after rotator cuff repair?
Some stiffness is common, and the AAOS lists it among known complications after rotator cuff surgery. Research reviews have found that earlier motion improves range of motion, but surgeons weigh this against protecting the repair. Stiffness that is not improving with your exercises should be reported to your surgeon and therapist so they can adjust your plan.
Can a repaired rotator cuff tear again?
Yes. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists re-tear as a complication of rotator cuff repair and notes the risk is higher with larger tears. Following your sling instructions, avoiding early lifting, and progressing exercises only when your surgeon allows all help protect the repair. New weakness or a painful pop after a fall or lift should be checked by your surgeon.
How long until I can use my arm normally after rotator cuff surgery?
According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, most people regain functional motion and adequate strength by 4 to 6 months after rotator cuff repair, and complete recovery can take several more months. Heavy lifting, overhead work and sports usually come last. Your surgeon decides when each activity is safe, based on healing and your progress in therapy.
