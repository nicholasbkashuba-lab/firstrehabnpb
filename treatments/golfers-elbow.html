Golfer's Elbow (Medial Epicondylitis)

Inner elbow pain that flares when you grip, lift or swing often eases with a gradual strengthening plan guided by a certified hand therapist.

Also called: medial epicondylitis, medial epicondylalgia, inner elbow tendinopathy, baseball elbow, suitcase elbow

Golfer's Elbow (Medial Epicondylitis), explained

Golfer's elbow, or medial epicondylitis, affects the tendons that anchor your forearm muscles to the bony bump on the inside of your elbow, called the medial epicondyle. These muscles bend your wrist and fingers and turn your palm downward. When they are overloaded again and again, the tendon develops small areas of damage and becomes sore, especially when you grip, lift or twist.

You do not need to play golf to get it. Cleveland Clinic notes it can come from hammering, carrying a loaded tray or long hours at a keyboard, and Johns Hopkins Medicine adds pitching, chopping wood and hauling heavy suitcases. A 2026 review in Cureus reports that it is most common in people aged 40 and older and that men and women are at similar risk.

Common symptoms

  • Pain and tenderness on the inside of the elbow
  • Pain that spreads along the palm side of the forearm toward the wrist
  • Pain when you grip, make a fist or bend your wrist against resistance
  • Elbow stiffness, often worse first thing in the morning
  • Weaker grip or wrist strength
  • Numbness or tingling that can travel into the fingers

Causes and risk factors

  • Repeated, forceful wrist bending, gripping and forearm turning
  • Repetitive arm tasks for two or more hours a day at work or in hobbies
  • Golf swings, throwing or racquet strokes with poor technique or little warm-up
  • Weak shoulder, wrist or forearm muscles
  • Age 40 or older
  • Smoking, obesity and diabetes

How hand therapy helps

Therapy sits at the center of conservative care. A 2026 review in Cureus describes eccentric loading of the forearm flexor tendons as particularly effective, with stretching and manual therapy adding complementary benefit. Mayo Clinic notes that surgery is rarely recommended and is considered only if symptoms have not improved after 6 to 12 months. At First Rehabilitation, golfer's elbow is treated by the hand therapy team led by Laura Drumm, CHT.

Graded tendon loading

Exercise starts gently for the wrist flexors and forearm pronators within your comfort, then moves to slow eccentric work as your pain allows, so the tendon is challenged without being overloaded.

Stretching and soft tissue work

Wrist flexor stretches and hands-on soft tissue techniques ease forearm tightness, which reviews describe as a useful partner to strengthening.

Strap and splint guidance

A counterforce strap worn just below the inner elbow can reduce load on the sore tendon. If your arm needs rest at night, a custom splint can be made in the clinic.

Whole-arm strengthening

Weak shoulder and wrist muscles are linked to golfer's elbow, so later phases build grip, wrist and shoulder strength to share the load during lifting and swinging.

Task and technique changes

Your therapist looks at how you grip tools, lift, type or swing, then adjusts technique, breaks and setup so daily tasks stop aggravating the tendon.

Ulnar nerve screening

The ulnar nerve runs right beside the sore tendon, so your therapist checks for tingling into the ring and little fingers and adjusts the plan if the nerve is involved.

What to expect at your first visit

Your evaluation takes about an hour, and treatment starts the same day. Your therapist asks when the pain began and which tasks set it off, then presses along the inner elbow to pinpoint tenderness. Resisted wrist bending and palm-down forearm turning show which tendons are involved, and grip strength is compared side to side. The ulnar nerve is screened for tingling, and you leave with a home program.

Recovery

Golfer's elbow tends to improve slowly. Cleveland Clinic advises resting the painful activity for at least four to six weeks, and Mayo Clinic notes that healing can take several months or longer. A 2026 Cureus review notes surgery is considered only after an extended period, typically 6 to 12 months, of non-surgical care. Your own timeline depends on how long symptoms have been present, how much you can reduce the aggravating load and how consistently you follow the home program.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Pause or cut back on the gripping, lifting or swinging that sets off your pain.
  • Place a wrapped ice pack on the inner elbow for 15 to 20 minutes, 3 to 4 times a day, as Mayo Clinic suggests.
  • Take short breaks during repetitive work and warm up before sports.
  • Once pain settles, return to activity gradually and practice the motion lightly before full effort.

When to see a doctor right away

  • Your elbow is swollen and warm to the touch and you have a fever
  • You cannot bend your elbow
  • Your elbow looks deformed or out of place
  • You think you may have broken a bone
  • Pain keeps going despite rest and physical therapy

Golfer's Elbow (Medial Epicondylitis): common questions

Is golfer's elbow the same as tennis elbow?
They are related but affect opposite sides of the elbow. Golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis) involves the tendons on the inner elbow that bend the wrist and turn the palm down. Tennis elbow involves the tendons on the outer elbow. A 2026 Cureus review reports that golfer's elbow accounts for about 10% of epicondylitis diagnoses, with tennis elbow far more common, and the exercises used in therapy differ for each.
Should I wear a brace for golfer's elbow?
A counterforce strap can help some people with golfer's elbow by reducing load where the tendon attaches. Mayo Clinic lists a forearm strap among self-care steps, and a 2026 review in Cureus says straps or night splints may offer some benefit by reducing load at the tendon origin. A brace works best as one part of a plan that also strengthens the forearm, so it is worth having a therapist check the fit and timing.
Can golfer's elbow cause numbness in my fingers?
It can. Mayo Clinic lists numbness or tingling that travels into the fingers as a possible symptom of golfer's elbow, and a 2026 Cureus review reports that up to 20% of patients have ulnar nerve involvement. The ulnar nerve passes right next to the inner elbow tendons. Tingling in the ring and little fingers deserves an evaluation, because nerve irritation changes how the elbow should be treated.
Do I have to stop golfing or lifting with golfer's elbow?
Mayo Clinic advises stopping the activities that cause pain while golfer's elbow settles, then returning slowly and practicing the motion before going back to full effort. Stopping does not have to be permanent. A therapist can help you find a modified version of your sport or workout, adjust grip or technique, and set checkpoints for adding load back in so the tendon keeps adapting.
Do cortisone shots cure golfer's elbow?
Cortisone injections can ease golfer's elbow pain for a short time. A 2026 Cureus review notes the effects are often short-lived and do not provide a long-term solution, and Mayo Clinic says cortisone injections are rarely recommended for this reason. Because the underlying problem is a tendon that cannot handle its current load, many physicians pair or replace injections with a structured exercise program. Talk with your physician about whether an injection makes sense for you.
Does Medicare cover hand therapy for golfer's elbow?
First Rehabilitation of North Palm Beach accepts Medicare and most major insurance plans for hand therapy, including care for golfer's elbow. Coverage details such as visit limits, copays and referral rules vary by plan, so the front desk verifies your benefits before your first visit.
