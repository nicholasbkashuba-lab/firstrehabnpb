Physical Therapy and Rehab Services in North Palm Beach: What We Treat

You tweaked your back lifting a kayak onto the roof rack. Your shoulder has ached since you started playing pickleball four mornings a week. Your knee…

You tweaked your back lifting a kayak onto the roof rack. Your shoulder has ached since you started playing pickleball four mornings a week. Your knee replacement was six weeks ago and the surgeon said to start therapy, but didn't say where. Or maybe nothing dramatic happened at all. You just noticed that getting up from the couch takes a little more effort than it used to, and you'd like to do something about it before it gets worse.

Whatever brought you here, the question is usually the same: is there somewhere close by that can actually help with this, and how soon can they see me?

This post walks through the conditions we see most and what treatment involves, then how the physical, occupational and hand therapy sides fit together under one roof on US-1.

By The First Rehabilitation Team · Reviewed by Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D.

The problems that fill our schedule

Physical therapy covers a lot of ground, so it helps to be specific. These are the problems we treat week in, week out.

Back and neck pain

Low back pain is one of the most common reasons people call us. Sometimes it's a disc, sometimes it's a strained muscle, and very often it's years of sitting at a desk with a monitor set two inches too low. Sciatica, the pain that travels from the back or buttock down the leg, is on the list too, and so is spinal stenosis. Neck pain and the headaches that come with it often trace back to the same habits. We figure out which it is before we treat it, and that sometimes means looking at your hips, since a stiff hip can masquerade as back pain.

Shoulders

Rotator cuff strains, tears and repairs, frozen shoulder, impingement, bursitis and tendinitis, and recovery after shoulder surgery, including reverse shoulder replacement. Shoulders don't tend to sort themselves out. They get stiffer and more guarded the longer you wait, and people start sleeping on the other side and reaching with the other arm without really noticing.

Knees and hips

Osteoarthritis, meniscus and ligament injuries, kneecap pain, hip bursitis, labral irritation, and the general wear that shows up after decades of walking around on them. We also work with people before surgery, so they go into a joint replacement stronger, and after it, from the first bend to full stride.

Hands, wrists and elbows

This is where our certified hand therapy program comes in. Laura Drumm, MS, OTR/L, CHT, CLT, leads it, and custom splints are made right in the clinic. Carpal tunnel, trigger finger, thumb arthritis, wrist fractures, tendon repairs and tennis elbow all live here.

Balance, falls and neurological rehab

If you've had a fall, or you've started holding the railing on stairs you used to take without thinking, tell us about it. Balance is trainable, and it responds to focused work. The same program covers vestibular work and recovery after a stroke (see how occupational therapy helps after a stroke), as well as conditions like Parkinson's and multiple sclerosis, working on gait, strength, coordination and the practical business of moving through a day safely.

Work injuries and car accidents

Repetitive strain, lifting injuries, whiplash and soft tissue damage after a crash. These come with adjusters, case managers and reporting requirements, and we handle that side of it too. After a car accident in Florida, your personal injury protection pays for medical care only if initial care starts within 14 days of the crash (section 627.736, 2026 Florida Statutes), and therapy is follow-up care a physician refers you to. So see a doctor first. Our page on therapy after a car accident explains how it works from there.

Golf, tennis and pickleball: the local wear and tear

North Palm Beach has a lot of people playing year-round, and we see the results: ankle sprains, hamstring strains, tennis and golfer's elbow, sore rotator cuffs, and the overuse injuries that come with repeating the same motion several thousand times a season.

Orthopedic shoulder surgeon Dr. Ryan Simovitch described the patient he worries about most on Episode 8 of our Pain 2 Power show, and it wasn't the weekend warrior. “I think it's the constant warrior,” he said, the person playing pickleball four to six days a week, where “there's no moderation, there's no preparation.”

Dr. Vani Sabesan, MD, an orthopaedic shoulder surgeon and the first female president of the Florida Orthopaedic Society, made the same point about our weather on Episode 9: in Florida “we have the indulgence of being able to play all year round.” She was also clear about how much the recovery side matters. “We really have to realize that rehab science in these sports is just as critical, probably maybe more critical than even, or as much of our surgical treatments.”

Golf has been part of the clinic's story for a long time. On that same episode Dave told the story of an early patient who said, “I'll do whatever it takes, but don't stop me from golfing.” He said that's what got him building golf and throwing programs. And tennis elbow, despite the name, is mostly not a tennis injury. Dr. Matt Butler, MD, a hand and upper extremity surgeon who was our guest on Episode 16, called it close to a rite of passage in middle age, and said that when he's the first stop for it, he usually sends the patient to Dave.

What treatment actually involves

A lot of people picture physical therapy as a room full of exercise bikes. Some of it is exercise, but the exercise is chosen deliberately, and it's rarely the whole picture.

Manual therapy

Hands-on work: joint mobilization, soft tissue release, stretching done with resistance we control. This is often what gets a stiff joint moving enough that the exercise part becomes possible. For a stubborn tennis elbow, Dave described on Episode 16 how that can include active release technique, blood flow restriction training and dynamic cupping.

Therapeutic exercise

Progressive strengthening and mobility work aimed at the specific weakness behind your problem, rather than general fitness. If your hip pain comes from a weak gluteus medius, that's what we train, with moves like banded sit to stands and clams. The exercises get harder as you get stronger, so you won't be doing the same routine on week six that you did on day one.

Gait and balance training

Retraining how you walk and how you recover when your weight shifts unexpectedly: a curb, a wet pool deck, a dog on a leash.

Modalities and massage

Heat and ice and electrical stimulation, used where they help with pain and inflammation. Therapeutic massage is offered alongside your therapy program too, as part of the session rather than an extra you book separately. All of these support the work rather than replace it.

Home exercise programs

The hour you spend with us matters less than what happens the other 167. We send you home with specific, manageable work, and we check that you can do it correctly before you leave. Our home exercises page has the knee, hip, shoulder and everyday sets Dave has described on air, with sets and reps.

Physical, occupational or hand therapy?

We're a Medicare-certified Comprehensive Outpatient Rehabilitation Facility, which in practice means physical therapy, occupational therapy and certified hand therapy happen in the same building, with the same team reading the same notes. Plenty of patients need more than one.

Roughly: physical therapy works on how you move, the strength, range and mechanics. Occupational therapy works on what you need to get done, from buttoning a shirt to getting back to your job. Hand therapy is the specialist end of both for the hand, wrist and elbow. You don't have to work out which you need before you call. The evaluation decides, and our post on PT versus OT explains the difference in more detail.

And when formal therapy ends, there's somewhere to go next. Our on-site wellness gym lets patients keep training with a team that already knows their history. It's a separate program that doesn't take insurance, so the front desk explains how it works before anyone signs up.

Your first visit, in brief

The first appointment is an evaluation, about an hour, one-on-one. We'll ask what's happening, when it started, what makes it worse, and what you're hoping to get back to, whether that's a golf swing or a full night's sleep without rolling onto the bad shoulder. Then we assess range of motion, strength, how you move, where it hurts and where it doesn't, and build a plan with realistic milestones. Treatment usually starts that same day.

If we think your problem needs a different kind of care, such as imaging, a surgical consult or a specialist, we'll say so. That's part of the evaluation too.

In most cases Florida's direct access law lets you start a physical therapy evaluation without a referral. Some plans still want one, and Medicare needs a physician to certify your plan of care. The front desk checks your plan and verifies your benefits before your first visit. For the full walkthrough, read what to expect at your first physical therapy visit, and if you're on Medicare, our 2026 Medicare guide covers deductibles and coinsurance.

Why close to home matters more than you'd think

Physical therapy is rarely a one-visit fix. Most plans involve regular visits over a number of weeks, and the exact count depends on your condition and how you respond. That's a lot of trips.

A clinic twenty-five minutes away looks reasonable on paper. By week three, when it's raining and traffic on US-1 is backed up, it's the reason people start skipping appointments, and consistent attendance is one of the biggest drivers of a good outcome. Choosing somewhere close to home or work is one of the more practical decisions you'll make about your care.
