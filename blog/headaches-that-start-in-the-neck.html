The Headache That Starts in Your Neck

Many stubborn, recurring headaches really start in the neck. Learn the signs, how physical therapy treats the driver, and when a headache needs a doctor first.

You know the headache before it fully arrives. It's the dull ache that creeps up from the base of your skull or settles behind one eye by mid-afternoon, then digs in for the rest of the day. You take something, it dulls the edge for a few hours, and by tomorrow the whole cycle starts over. That gets exhausting when the pills only ever seem to mask the problem instead of ending it. For many stubborn, recurring headaches, the source sits a few inches lower, in the neck.

By The First Rehabilitation Team · Reviewed by Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D.

What “the neck as a headache source” actually means

The upper part of your neck and the base of your skull share a crowded, sensitive neighborhood of joints, muscles and nerves. When the small joints at the very top of the spine get stiff, or the muscles that run from your shoulders up into the back of your head stay tight and overworked, the pain they produce doesn't always stay put. It can be felt as head pain: across the forehead, around one eye, or wrapping the back of the skull. That's what clinicians mean by referred pain. The trouble is in the neck, but you feel it up top.

Cervicogenic headache

A cervicogenic headache is one driven directly by the neck's joints and tissues. It's often felt on one side, and it often comes paired with a stiff, cranky neck that you may have stopped noticing because it's been that way for months.

Tension-type headache

A tension-type headache tends to feel like a band of pressure around the head, and it frequently rides along with the same tight muscles and locked-up posture. In real life the two blur together. Plenty of people have a bit of both.

The everyday cause: sustained position

The usual culprit for either one is boringly ordinary: hours on a phone or a workday hunched toward a screen, or a long drive with your head pushed forward. Each one asks the upper neck to hold a load it was never built to hold all day. The joints stiffen and the muscles guard, and then the head pain follows.

Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D., OTR, our founder, described the slow version of this on Episode 8 of our Pain 2 Power show: “as we get older, the tendency for us to have forward positioning, even the head forward positioning… that's just gravity, that's how life is.” Gravity you can't do much about. How long you sit still in front of it is a different story.

Signs your headache might be neck-driven

No article can diagnose you, so treat these patterns as clues for a professional to evaluate. A headache is more likely to have a neck component when it tends to start at the base of the skull or on one side, rather than deep in the center of the head. It often builds through the day instead of hitting all at once, and it tends to get worse after a long stretch at a desk or after a drive.

Another telling pattern is what changes it. Neck-driven headaches frequently ease when you change position or stand up and move, and they flare again when you settle back into the same posture. They often travel with a companion, too: tightness or soreness through the neck and shoulders. If several of these sound familiar, that's a reason to get looked at.

Where the neck and the headache meet in daily life

When we sit down with a headache patient, a lot of the detective work is about the day they actually live, because that's where the load comes from.

At the desk

Laptop low, screen off to one side, phone wedged between ear and shoulder for a call. Any of those, held for an afternoon, keeps the upper neck working overtime. Part of our care is workstation coaching: where the screen sits, how you sit, and how often you get up. Nothing fancy. It just has to match how you work.

Behind the wheel, and after a crash

A long drive down I-95 or US-1 with your chin pushed toward the windshield is a classic setup. A car accident is a different matter. Headaches after whiplash or a concussion are among the conditions our headache care covers, but a physician should see you first. In Florida that's also about coverage: personal injury protection pays for medical care only if initial care starts within 14 days of the crash, and physical therapy is follow-up care your physician refers you to. Our car accident rehab page explains the rule.

In bed

Sleep position counts too. Some people wake up with the headache already on board, which tells us something about the night. Sleep-position coaching is part of how we treat neck pain, and the same conversation applies here. So does the jaw: jaw and postural contributions to head pain are on the list of things we look at.

What physical therapy actually does about it

The point of therapy here is to treat the driver, rather than only quiet the symptom.

It starts with listening

Your first visit runs about an hour, one-on-one with your therapist, and treatment usually begins that day. Before anyone touches your neck, we take your history: when the headaches come, which side, what you were doing, what helps. Dave makes a point of this. What you tell us shapes the whole assessment, and on Episode 9 he said people don't realize how important it is “to have your therapist take really good notes and listen.” Then we watch how your neck moves and measure motion, strength and the positions that bring the pain on. The first visit page has the rest of the details.

Hands-on work first

Care usually starts with skilled manual therapy. A therapist assesses the specific joints of the upper neck, finds the stiff or irritable ones, and uses gentle techniques to restore how they move, while releasing the muscles that have been guarding around them. That alone can take pressure off the structures that refer pain into the head.

Then the reasons it keeps coming back

Lasting change usually depends on the next step: addressing why the tension keeps rebuilding. That means working on the posture and strength gaps that let your head drift forward and your neck do all the work. Expect strengthening for the deep neck flexors and the upper back, opening up the mid-back and shoulders, and adjustments to how you sit, drive and hold your phone. Most neck programs borrow from the same small set of exercises.

What you can do between visits

Therapy visits are a few hours a week. Your neck lives with you for the other hundred and sixty-odd. On Episode 13, Dave said he sees people two to three times a week, and the rest of the time they need to be doing their exercises, and doing them correctly. If ten repetitions are prescribed, fifty is not a bonus.

He's also got a favorite observation about mornings. Babies stretch when they wake up, and so do dogs and cats, before they do anything at all. Most of us grab our glasses and a coffee and head straight for the desk or the car, then wonder why the neck is stiff by lunchtime. We wrote more about that first hour of the day in five morning habits for back pain, and most of it applies to necks too.

One more thing that helps us help you. Before your evaluation, jot down a week of notes on your headaches:

  • What time each one started, and where you felt it first
  • Which side, and whether it was the same side every time
  • What you'd been doing for the hour before (desk, drive, phone, sleep)
  • What eased it, and what made it worse
  • Any medication you took for it

That's the history Dave was talking about, written down while it's fresh. It makes the first visit sharper.

When a headache needs a doctor first

Physical therapy is a strong option for headaches that come from the neck, but not every headache belongs in that category, and some need medical care right away. A sudden, severe headache that feels like the worst of your life is an emergency and calls for urgent evaluation, not a therapy appointment. So does a headache that arrives with fever, confusion, vision loss, weakness, numbness, or trouble speaking, or one that follows a significant blow to the head.

If a headache is new, changing in a way that worries you, or simply different from your usual pattern, the responsible first move is to be checked by a physician. Getting the serious causes ruled out first is what makes it safe to then treat the mechanical, neck-driven piece with confidence. And if we see something during your evaluation that belongs with a doctor, we'll tell you so.

Finding out what's behind yours

If your headaches keep circling back and the medicine only ever buys you a few hours, find out whether your neck is part of the story. A thorough evaluation can tell us what's driving your pain and whether hands-on care, posture work and a simple home plan are likely to help. Most of our neck pain patients notice meaningful change within the first few weeks of consistent care. You can read more on our headache treatment page, and because the neck is so often the engine of the problem, on how we treat neck pain. Both live under our broader physical therapy services.

In most cases Florida's direct access law lets you start a physical therapy evaluation without a physician referral; Medicare does require a physician to certify your plan of care, and our front desk verifies your benefits before your first visit.
