Strength Training After 60: Where to Start

Why sitting is the quiet risk as you age, and what a short daily movement habit plus supervised strength work do for fall risk and independence.

Somewhere between fifty and sixty, gravity starts winning arguments it used to lose. The shoulders round forward, the head drifts ahead of the spine, and most people blame age instead of the eight or ten hours a day they spend sitting down.

By The First Rehabilitation Team · Reviewed by Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D.

This post is for the person who has noticed it starting. You stand up from the couch in two tries instead of one. You hold the rail on the stairs at the condo even though nobody told you to. A walk along the Intracoastal you used to love now feels like something to schedule around. None of that is an emergency, but it tells you something, and the earlier you act on it, the more there is to keep.

The chair is doing more damage than the years are

“The invention of the chair is one of our demise,” says Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D., founder of First Rehabilitation and an occupational therapist who has treated patients since 1991. He means what happens to a body that spends most of the day in one position: forward head posture, tight hips, and blood flow that slows down instead of circulating. “That's just gravity, that's how life is,” he says of the forward drift that comes with age, “but we want to walk every day, we want to try to get the blood flowing.”

Think about a normal day for a lot of our patients. Coffee in the chair by the window. The drive down US-1 to an appointment. Lunch at a table, an afternoon with a book or a screen, dinner, then the recliner for the evening news. Every one of those is reasonable. Stack them together and the legs barely work from breakfast to bed.

That matters more in this part of Florida than most. Two thirds of the people we treat are 65 or older, which is why our front desk spends so much of the day on Medicare questions. Danielle Armstrong, who owns Pilates Life on Northlake in Palm Beach Gardens, described the same crowd on Episode 18 of our Pain 2 Power show. People here, she said, “are looking for that longevity,” and what they really want from exercise is help with ordinary life: “Things like reaching for something on a shelf or bringing their garbage out or bringing in groceries.”

Ten minutes counts, and it beats waiting for an hour you never get

The advice Dave gives patients starts small, on purpose. “Just a little bit of exercises, ten, fifteen minutes a day, three minutes a day, and work your way up,” he says. Put the phone down, get up, walk, do a few sit to stands, and repeat that through the day rather than saving it for one long session. The goal is motion that happens often. Perfect form on a Saturday morning matters less than ordinary movement on a Tuesday afternoon.

What “a little bit” looks like in a real day

Three minutes is not a typo. If walking to the mailbox and back is where you are, then that is the starting line, and the job is to do it again after lunch and again before dinner. Stand up during every commercial break. Do five sit to stands before you sit down to eat. Walk the length of the parking lot at Publix instead of circling for the closest spot. Each piece is tiny. Spread across a week, they add up to a habit your legs notice.

Dave's list on our home exercises page calls this the baseline, the floor you build everything else on.

Sit to stands, the test you can do in your kitchen

Rising from a chair without using your hands is one of the simplest tests of leg strength there is, and it is the same movement that keeps someone able to get off a low couch, a car seat, or the toilet without help. Losing it rarely announces itself. It slips a little at a time until a fall makes it obvious.

The same movement is also the exercise. Our therapists use three versions of it, and all three are on the exercises page with the sets and reps Dave gives patients.

If any of these causes sharp pain, or you feel unsteady doing them, stop. That is a sign to get evaluated first, which we come back to below.

Balance and strength are the same project

Most falls have a long run-up. Hips get a little weaker and reaction time slows down, without anyone noticing. Then one day the curb outside the restaurant is an inch higher than you expected. Supervised strength training after 60 works on both halves at once: the muscle that holds you up, and the balance reactions that catch you when something shifts under your feet.

Start before there is a problem

Danielle Armstrong made the point on Episode 18 that balance work belongs early, before the first scare. “You want to start doing it early so that you know how to keep strong,” she said. “Prevention is better than a cure.” Mike McGann, Dave's co-host, put it in the language of daily life: balance and muscle strength are what make walking, taking the garbage out, and getting in and out of the shower or the bathtub safe.

Our glossary describes balance training as exercises that challenge your steadiness on purpose, like standing on one foot while reaching. Done with someone beside you, that challenge is safe. Done alone on a slippery tile floor, it can be the very thing you were trying to avoid.

Sleep and recovery count too

Elyse Marrone, RD/LDN, CGP, a registered dietitian who sees patients in Jupiter and joined Danielle on the same episode, said this about deep sleep: “I know you can increase it by exercise.” Her goal for patients is one most of ours would sign up for: “I want to be healthy, healthy, healthy, dead.” In other words, stay capable for as long as possible instead of sliding slowly into decline. Strength and balance are a big part of the capable half.

If pain is why you have been sitting more

Sometimes the sitting is protection rather than habit. A bad hip, knee, or back makes standing and walking hurt, so the chair starts to feel like the safe choice, and the muscles that would have supported those joints get weaker from disuse. We see this constantly in patients recovering from hip pain, knee pain, and back pain, and it is exactly the cycle a good evaluation is built to interrupt: treat the pain that is driving the inactivity, then rebuild the strength that inactivity cost.

Picture how it usually goes. You stop your morning walk because a knee aches on the way back. Within a season, getting out of the car takes two hands on the door frame. The knee was the first problem. The weaker legs became the second one, and by the time most people call us, the second one is the bigger of the two.

Patients from Juno Beach and the surrounding area often ask us the same question at discharge: what now? The exercises do not stop being useful once the pain does. They become the maintenance plan.

What a fall prevention evaluation looks for

If you have already had a fall, or a near miss on the stairs, start with your doctor and then an evaluation rather than the gym. The same goes for new dizziness, a recent diagnosis, or a fall you cannot explain. Balance and fall prevention are part of our physical therapy program, alongside vestibular work for dizziness and neurological rehab.

At the first visit, which runs about an hour, your therapist watches you walk, stand and turn, and measures strength, range of motion and balance. A fall-risk screening is often part of it: short tests like standing up from a chair and walking a short distance while the therapist times you. The plan then targets the specific weakness behind the wobble instead of guessing at it, and treatment usually starts that same day.

Every session after that is one-on-one. That matters for balance work in particular, because the exercises that help the most are the ones that feel a little uncertain, and you want a trained person within arm's reach when they do.

Does Medicare cover this, and what about the gym?

Coverage is simpler than most people expect. Medicare covers medically necessary physical therapy, and that includes skilled care that helps you hold on to your function or slow a decline, not only care that keeps producing gains. People are often told the opposite. Our 2026 Medicare guide explains the rule, the deductible, and what you pay per visit. Medicare does need a physician to certify your plan of care, and we handle that paperwork with your doctor. The front desk verifies your benefits before the first visit, so nothing ambushes you.

The flip side is that skilled therapy is the right tool only while you need a therapist's skills. Once a home program you can do safely on your own will keep your progress, we will tell you so. That is where the next step comes in.

Where the on-site gym fits

Our on-site wellness program picks up where formal therapy leaves off. Patients who finish physical or occupational therapy with us can keep training in the same building, with a team that already knows their history, instead of starting over with a trainer who has never seen their chart. Senior functional fitness here is about the strength and balance that keep people independent. Nobody is chasing heavier plates on a bar.

The program covers a range of paces on purpose. Some people want one-on-one personal training built around a specific goal or a joint we already know well. Others do better in a small group class, where it is easier to stay consistent when someone else is expecting you. The gym does not take insurance, and it is a separate program from Medicare-covered therapy. The front desk explains how it works and what it costs before you sign up.

Book an evaluation

If you have noticed yourself avoiding stairs or sitting more than you would like to admit, an evaluation is the place to start, ahead of any gym membership you are not sure you are ready for.
