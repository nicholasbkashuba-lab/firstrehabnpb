The Muscle Comes Off Too: Paul Joyce on GLP-1s and Peptides

Paul Joyce of New Life HRT joins Dave and Mike on peptides, GLP-1s and hormone therapy, why the weight takes muscle with it, and what six lab tests found.

Paul Joyce has a completely torn rotator cuff and no plans to have it repaired. He plays golf most days, and the swing itself does not bother him. What used to be excruciating was turning the steering wheel on the drive over.

He had a shoulder replacement on the schedule and cancelled it. Now he comes to Dave instead. That is how this week’s Pain 2 Power opened, before turning into an hour on peptides, GLP-1s and hormone replacement therapy with the man a lot of doctors learn this material from.

By The First Rehabilitation Team · Reviewed by Dr. Dave Kashuba, Ph.D.

Paul runs New Life HRT and has been a friend of Dave’s for 22 years. If you have a neighbor at the club on a weight loss shot, or you have wondered about one yourself, this is the episode to hear. Below is the part of it that matters to a rehab clinic, plus where we stand on the rest.

What a peptide actually is

Mike started where these conversations should start and almost never do. What is a peptide? Paul Joyce, who runs New Life HRT, gave the short version. “A peptide is a chain of amino acids. A short chain of amino acids, usually under 50, that are bound together,” he said. They carry messages about repairing injury, losing weight, and mood, and he counts around 150 of them.

From the gut to the Gila monster

Dave filled in where the weight loss drugs came from. GLP-1 stands for glucagon like peptide, which your gut already makes, though your own clears too fast to do much. The version in the medications was modeled on a compound found in the Gila monster, which stays in the body long enough to keep working. And no, Paul confirmed, you are not injecting lizard venom. He gets asked constantly.

Paul has been at this longer than most. He began prescribing Ozempic off label for weight loss back in 2017, well before the rest of the country caught on, and he now trains physicians on peptide and hormone replacement protocols before they offer them in their own practices.

The weight comes off with muscle attached

Dave sees people well into a course of a GLP-1 who have lost the weight and started to look frail, because nothing else about how they eat or train has changed. “It’s going to make you better, but it’s not gonna make you healthier if you don’t do something more with it,” he said.

Paul agreed flatly. “You definitely have to make sure you’re getting enough protein,” he said, and when Dave asked whether the loss includes muscle, the answer was “Yeah, you lose a lot of muscle.” He was describing himself as much as his patients. He started at 209 pounds, weighs 153 now, and says he took it too far. He stopped about a month before the show and his appetite is still catching up.

Why the muscle matters more after 60

Lean mass matters more the older you are. Strength is what gets you off a low couch and back onto your feet after a stumble. Lose enough and a trip that used to be nothing becomes a fall. Two thirds of the people we treat are 65 or older. For them, keeping muscle is about staying independent, and looks barely come into it.

Holding onto muscle while the scale moves is ordinary work: resistance training and protein. The first half of that is what our wellness gym and physical therapy teams do all day. Something as plain as standing up from a chair without your hands, a few times a day, is a decent start, and Dave’s version of it is on our home exercises page. Our earlier post on strength training after 60 covers where to begin.

Food first, says the dietitian

Protein is the half we leave to the people who do it for a living. On Episode 18, registered dietitian Elyse Marrone, RD/LDN, CGP, who sees patients in Jupiter, laid out the seven pillars she works through with every patient: food first, exercise, sleep, stress management, supplementation, hydration and community. She did not talk about GLP-1s on that show. Her general point still lands here, because bloodwork follows habits: “the labs are going to change based on those seven pillars of health,” she said. A shot can move the scale. The food and the training decide what the weight loss is made of.

Six websites, six sets of problems

Then the talk turned to where people buy this stuff. Search for a peptide online and you will find it sold cheap, labeled not for human consumption, for research purposes only. Paul had six of those sites tested.

All six came back with flaws he called major. Heavy metals in some. One carried a low level of bacteria. One was sold as retatrutide and turned out to be underdosed semaglutide, a different drug altogether. He has since served as an expert witness in an Atlanta case against a company using a fabricated certificate of analysis, the document these sellers hold up to look legitimate and, he says, often just invent.

The regulated end of the supply chain looks different. Everything he uses comes from a 503A or 503B compounding pharmacy, FDA inspected and DEA licensed. Of every thousand vials produced, a hundred go to an independent lab for sterility and potency testing.

Do people just skip the doctor?

The hosts asked Paul straight out whether people simply go on these things without seeking professional help. His answer was short. “Well, a lot of people go on it,” he said, and the place they get it is those same research only websites. Put that next to the six test results and you can see why he spent so much of the hour on sourcing.

Is there any proof it works?

To his credit, Paul did not oversell it. The question put to him on air was the skeptic’s one: when someone is at their worst, in terrible pain with nothing working, and they start a peptide, how do you know they were not about to get better anyway? And have there been actual studies?

“It’s not FDA approved,” Paul said. “There’s been studies done mostly on animals, however, there has been some human studies.” What he leans on instead is experience: taking it himself, and what he says he has seen in a thousand patients who have used it. That is an honest description of where the evidence sits. It is also a good reason to keep a physician in the loop, and that is where we land too.

Three weeks without pain, still no strength

Back to that shoulder. Paul started BPC-157 while the surgery was still on the calendar, and within about three weeks, on his account, the pain was gone. The strength did not come with it. “But I’m still weak. I have no strength in my shoulder,” he said. “I can’t even lift my arm up some ways. But there’s no pain anymore.”

Golf every day, but not the steering wheel

His reason for cancelling the replacement will make sense to half the golfers in Palm Beach County. “I’m an addicted golfer. I literally try to play golf every day, but the golf swing doesn’t hurt my shoulder,” he said. “It’s the craziest thing.” Surgery, as he understood it, meant eight months without a club in his hands, and he was not willing to give that up while the swing still worked.

We meet that gap every week. A cuff that is fully torn does not knit itself back together because it stopped hurting, so pain leaving is the moment to start rehab rather than skip it. Paul took the first road. “So I started going to Dave, and it’s been a Godsend,” he said. “My shoulder is, like, a lot better.” Earlier in that answer he said the pain had diminished greatly, adding, “I have more range of motion.”

What rehab looks like when the tear stays torn

Our shoulder rehab follows a familiar order: get back pain-free range of motion first, then rebuild strength in the muscles around the cuff and the shoulder blade, then return to reaching, lifting, sport and sleep. With a tear that will not close, the muscles that still work have to carry more of the load, so that middle step gets most of the attention. Every session is one-on-one.

Whether a cuff is intact also shapes surgery if it ever comes back on the table. Orthopedic shoulder surgeon Dr. Ryan Simovitch explained on Episode 8 that the state of the rotator cuff decides which kind of shoulder replacement a person gets, and our post on total versus reverse shoulder replacement walks through why.

Where we stand on peptides and hormones

BPC-157 is not FDA approved. Paul said so himself on air, and we do not prescribe, sell or advise on any of it. Peptides and hormone replacement belong in a conversation with a physician who runs your bloodwork. What comes after, the strength and the movement, is ours.

Elyse Marrone described her own line the same way on Episode 18. “I don’t prescribe hormones but I work with a lot of providers who do,” she said. That division of labor is how it should work. The prescriber handles the prescription, the dietitian handles the plate, and a therapist handles the shoulder that still will not lift and the legs that got weaker while the scale went down.
