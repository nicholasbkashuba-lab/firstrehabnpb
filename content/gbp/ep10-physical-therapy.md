Months of back treatment, and nobody asked about your groin.

That is the gap where hip impingement hides. The hip is a ball in a socket, and when the two stop clearing each other cleanly they collide at the edges instead of gliding. The pain lands low in the back, so the back gets treated and the hip never gets checked.

The detail that separates them is simple. A disc problem tends to send pain down the leg. A hip problem sends it into the groin.

Other things worth noticing:

• Aching toward the buttock or over the SI joint
• Soreness on the outside of the hip
• Pain with anything that twists under load

keyword: hip pain physical therapy Palm Beach Gardens

Your gluteus medius is probably the reason your hip hurts.

It is the muscle on the side of the hip that keeps your pelvis level while you stand on one leg. Every step you take is briefly a single leg stance. When that muscle quits, the joint absorbs the load the muscle should have carried.

The exercise we start most people on is a sit to stand with a resistance band looped just above the knees. The band is the whole point. A sore hip makes you quietly shift your weight onto the good leg, and you will do it for weeks without noticing. The band pushes both knees outward and forces the two sides to share the work.

From there the program builds. Standing leg abductions holding the back of a chair. Clams to wake up the external rotators, which are weak in almost everybody because nothing in daily life asks for them. A prone stretch to open the front of the hip.

keyword: golf hip pain treatment Jupiter FL

Golfers do not have to quit golf.

Hip impingement flares with torque, and a golf swing is torque by design. That is why the pain shows up on the course before it shows up anywhere else, and why so many players assume the only options are playing through it or putting the clubs away.

There is a third option. Change the swing so it stops driving rotation through the hip, then build the strength that lets the joint tolerate what is left. Tennis and pickleball players get the same treatment for the same reason.

A few habits help while you are at it:

• Stop sitting with your legs crossed
• Break up long stretches of repetitive bending
• Warm the hips before the first tee, not after the turn

Think of the labrum as a gasket.

That is how orthopedic surgeon Dr. Zach McVicker described it on our Pain 2 Power podcast. The labrum sits around the rim of the hip socket and creates a suction seal, and that seal spreads your body weight across the whole dome of the joint every time you take a step.

Lose the seal and the load stops being shared. It concentrates on cartilage that was never built to carry it that way, which is why a labral problem tends to hurt with every single step rather than only at the end of a long day.

It is also why waiting is expensive. The joint keeps grinding at itself while you decide, and the repair gets harder the longer the seal stays broken.

Therapy is where this should start, and for a lot of people it is where it ends. Strengthening the muscles that support the hip takes load off the joint surface and often settles symptoms without anyone going near an operating room. When it does need a surgeon, Dave sends people to one he trusts.

Close your eyes. Have someone lift one of your fingers. Do you know it moved?

That sense is called proprioception, and it is how your body tracks where it is without looking. People lose it, particularly in the legs, and losing it is what turns an ordinary step into a misstep. Missteps become falls, and a broken hip in an older adult starts a decline that is hard to reverse.

It is also one of the reasons we push people toward preserving their own hip rather than replacing it when preservation is still on the table. A replacement can feel close to normal, but it does not give back the positional sense a native joint has.

Balance and strength work is a core part of physical therapy here in North Palm Beach. We look at how steady you are on one leg, how well your hips hold your pelvis level, and how quickly you recover when your weight shifts unexpectedly, then train the gaps.
